# Structural Crime Scene Processing

## Role-Pair and Chain Handoff Packet Examples

## Draft 1

---

## Purpose

This document adds completed handoff packet examples for the role-pair and chain structures introduced in the mixed-role certification stack.

The mixed-role certification companion defines the packet grammar.

The role-specific exercise, scoring, threshold, signoff, and completed-example companions show how individual roles should perform before and during those transfers.

This companion answers the next practical question.

It shows what a completed adjacent-role or chain handoff packet should actually look like once evaluators and learners have finished the packet and recorded a bounded cross-role decision.

Its purpose is to make the handoff layer concrete enough for teaching, calibration, certification, and deployment planning.

---

## What This Document Does

This companion provides completed example packets for:

- patrol to scene technician adjacent-role handoff
- scene technician to detective transfer
- detective to laboratory structured request chain
- detective, supervisor, and prosecutor transfer review
- compressed four-role operational chain
- mixed-role failure reentry and recertification

The examples use the grammar already established in the mixed-role certification companion.

Across the packet set, this document models:

- a completed master mixed-role certification cover sheet
- completed cross-role clearance review sheets
- completed role-to-role convergence notes
- completed failed-handoff diagnosis where needed
- completed conditional, restriction, or remediation logic where needed
- promotion and bridge logic where the packet requires it

These are not blank templates.

They are completed packet models meant to show what strong, bounded cross-role documentation looks like.

## How to Use These Packet Examples

Use these packets in four ways:

1. as instructor models before mixed-role drill work begins
2. as debrief comparison artifacts after a handoff or chain run
3. as calibration references when evaluators disagree about whether transfer was actually safe
4. as implementation references when building local mixed-role certification kits

Do not copy the wording mechanically into unrelated cases.

Treat these as examples of packet discipline, bounded language, and role-to-role accountability.

---

## Conventions Used in These Examples

These packets are written in concise operational language.

When a packet is clear, it says why.

When a packet is restricted, it says where the transfer failed.

When the team left residue, the residue stays visible in the decision rather than being buried under a smoother summary.

That is part of the model.

## Cross-Packet Reading Notes

Across these packets, three patterns matter.

First, a pass is not the same thing as a broad pass.

Several packets are intentionally bounded because the team is usable only inside a named scope.

Second, strong packets do not sound smoother because they hide residue.

They sound cleaner because the residue is better located.

Third, the decisive question in every packet is the same.

Did one role leave the next role with a safer thinking problem or a dirtier one.

That is the actual handoff standard.

---

## Common Misreadings

### Misreading 1: A completed handoff packet should look like team consensus

Not necessarily.

A strong packet may still include visible disagreement, restriction, or conditional clearance.

### Misreading 2: If the downstream role understood the upstream role eventually, the handoff was fine

Not if understanding required oral repair.

That is exactly what these packets are supposed to detect.

### Misreading 3: Conditional clearance means the packet was weak

Not always.

Conditional clearance can be the correct structural decision when the boundary is usable but not yet durable under pressure.

### Misreading 4: Failure reentry should sound more confident than the original packet

No.

It should sound more governed.

### Misreading 5: These packets replace the mixed-role certification forms

They do not.

They model completed use of those forms.
