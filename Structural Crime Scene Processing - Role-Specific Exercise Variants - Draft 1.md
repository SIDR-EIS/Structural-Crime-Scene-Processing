# Structural Crime Scene Processing

## Role-Specific Exercise Variants

## Draft 1

---

## Purpose

The base exercise book gives the project a general training layer.

This companion adds the next operational layer.

It turns the same underlying scenarios into role-specific drills so that patrol, scene technicians, detectives, laboratory personnel, supervisors, and downstream legal teams each train against the failure pressures that actually belong to their role.

A generic exercise is useful when the goal is to teach the language of the method.

A role-specific exercise is necessary when the goal is to change behavior under pressure.

This document is therefore not a replacement for `Structural Crime Scene Processing - Case Exercises - Draft 1.md` or for `Structural Crime Scene Processing - Courtroom Transfer and Legal Review Scenarios - Draft 1.md`.

It is the conversion layer between those shared scenarios and role-specific retraining.

## How to Use This Companion

Each exercise variant should be run with four explicit constraints:

1. The learner only receives the information that role would realistically have at that stage.
2. The learner must produce outputs in the grammar of that role rather than in generic case-summary prose.
3. The facilitator must evaluate both correctness and role-discipline.
4. Pressure conditions should be added once the learner can handle the clean version honestly.

For most cohorts, the right sequence is:

1. teach the general scenario
2. run the role-specific variant
3. compare outputs across roles
4. run a mixed-role handoff or review drill
5. only then introduce timing pressure or public-pressure conditions

If role-specific drills are taught before the shared structural grammar is learned, the agency will simply recreate local dialects.

## Standard Variant Structure

Every exercise in this companion uses the same six-part frame:

- `Base scenario`: the source scenario or legal packet
- `Role exposure setup`: what that role is told, shown, or handed
- `Training objective`: the specific retraining target
- `Learner tasks`: what the learner must do
- `Required outputs`: what must be produced in packet or memo form
- `Evaluator focus`: what the facilitator should watch for

Optional `Pressure switches` are included for later runs.

## Role-to-Scenario Matrix

| Role | Primary source scenarios | Best first emphasis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Patrol / First Response | Apartment Hallway Collapse, Office Quiet Death, Warehouse Loading Bay | boundary, disturbance, handoff |
| Scene Technicians | Apartment Hallway Collapse, Office Quiet Death, Warehouse Loading Bay | anchors, graph, ranked collection |
| Detectives | Apartment Hallway Collapse, Office Quiet Death, Warehouse Loading Bay | witness disaggregation, hypotheses, residue |
| Laboratory Personnel | Apartment Hallway Collapse, Office Quiet Death, Warehouse Loading Bay | intake, sequencing, null-result honesty |
| Supervisors / Review Boards | outputs from all three base scenarios | closure review, false stability, redesign |
| Prosecutors / Downstream Legal Teams | legal-review scenario set plus integrated package excerpts | bounded legal claims, transfer fragility, reentry |

The same scenario should often be run across multiple roles in sequence.

That is how the institution learns that one role's convenience becomes another role's instability.

---

## Recommended Program Order

A strong first cycle looks like this:

1. Patrol variants
2. Scene technician variants
3. Detective variants
4. Laboratory variants
5. Supervisor variants
6. Prosecutor variants
7. Mixed-role handoff drills

That order roughly tracks the movement of the case through the system.

It also makes it easier for later roles to learn how earlier roles create or destroy the conditions they inherit.

## Part I. Patrol and First Response Variants

### Role focus

Patrol variants should be built to punish premature interpretation, invisible disturbance, and weak handoff.

The patrol learner should leave the drill with the feeling that the first minutes are not a preface to the case.

They are a decisive structural phase of the case.

### Patrol Variant 1. Dispatch-to-Arrival Discipline

`Base scenario`: Exercise 1. Apartment Hallway Collapse

`Role exposure setup`:

The patrol learner receives only a dispatch line, a time stamp, apartment number, and the fact that a person is reported down in a hallway outside a unit. Two neighbors are visible on arrival. EMS is en route. No one has separated the witnesses.

`Training objective`:

Force the learner to distinguish life-safety action, disturbance logging, and interpretive restraint in the first three minutes.

`Learner tasks`:

- state the first boundary set they would create
- identify likely disturbance points before they occur
- name what must be recorded if the body or surrounding items are touched for medical access
- separate what is known on arrival from what is merely being said nearby

`Required outputs`:

- Form 1 excerpt
- Form 3 arrival-state excerpt
- Form 4 first-anchor excerpt
- a short patrol handoff statement for Form 12

`Evaluator focus`:

Watch for whether the learner documents responder-caused change, witness proximity, access uncertainty, and open scene perimeter questions.

The learner fails if they start explaining the collapse before they have stabilized the first-contact record.

`Pressure switches`:

- loud family member demanding entry
- supervisor asking if this looks accidental
- EMT insisting on rapid corridor clearing

### Patrol Variant 2. Witness Separation and Narrative Containment

`Base scenario`: Exercise 2. Office Quiet Death

`Role exposure setup`:

The learner arrives to a quiet office scene where one coworker is talking continuously, another is crying, and a manager is already offering a timeline. The body is in a chair. Nothing looks obviously violent.

`Training objective`:

Train the learner to resist category closure from calmness, status, or early managerial narrative.

`Learner tasks`:

- describe how they would separate witnesses and control scene language
- identify what arrival-state details are at greatest risk of being overwritten by office traffic
- record what is visually stable versus what needs immediate confirmation
- decide what should be deferred rather than improvised

`Required outputs`:

- Form 1 excerpt
- Form 2 patrol-phase closure excerpt
- Form 10 deferral entries
- Form 12 patrol section

`Evaluator focus`:

Look for whether the learner confuses calm scene presentation with analytical closure.

The learner should preserve ambiguity, not eliminate it.

`Pressure switches`:

- executive requests a quick cause classification
- human-resources representative enters the scene area
- building security claims the office was empty overnight without documentation

### Patrol Variant 3. Blind Handoff Under Mixed Agency Traffic

`Base scenario`: Exercise 3. Warehouse Loading Bay Reconciliation Failure

`Role exposure setup`:

The learner is the first patrol unit at a warehouse loading area with private security, site management, and a transport contractor already moving around the space. The learner has partial radio traffic and incomplete access information.

`Training objective`:

Make the learner preserve handoff quality when the scene is spatially large, socially busy, and already partially disturbed.

`Learner tasks`:

- establish initial inner and outer boundaries
- identify which people must be logged as already inside the scene field
- name the perishable states most likely to be lost in a loading environment
- produce a handoff that tells the next role what patrol does not know

`Required outputs`:

- Form 1 excerpt
- Form 3 transition sketch
- Form 10 deferral entries
- Form 12 handoff note

`Evaluator focus`:

The best patrol answer makes inherited uncertainty visible.

The worst answer sounds organized but hides scene traffic, access ambiguity, and contamination history.

`Pressure switches`:

- rain starts affecting the loading area
- site manager demands partial reopening of the bay
- second unit assumes the event is only a logistics discrepancy

### Patrol progression note

Run these three variants before any timed patrol certification.

If patrol cannot keep structure under calm, noisy, and mixed-agency conditions, later role drills will just train compensation.

## Part II. Scene Technician Variants

### Role focus

Technician variants should be built to punish collection-first reflexes and reward anchor, graph, and discriminating-value discipline.

### Technician Variant 1. First-Ten-Minute Anchor Capture

`Base scenario`: Exercise 1. Apartment Hallway Collapse

`Role exposure setup`:

The technician inherits a hallway scene already touched by patrol and EMS. Some movement is documented, some is not. Neighbors have been pushed back but the corridor is narrow and contaminated by traffic.

`Training objective`:

Train the technician to stabilize anchor hierarchy before defaulting into item recovery.

`Learner tasks`:

- identify the strongest immediately recoverable anchors
- distinguish scene-native conditions from responder-created changes
- name what must be measured or photographed before collection begins
- state which tempting items are low priority despite visual salience

`Required outputs`:

- Form 4 anchor hierarchy excerpt
- Form 5 scene graph sketch
- Form 7 first ranked collection block

`Evaluator focus`:

The learner should show a deliberate anchor order and an explicit refusal to let dramatic detail outrank structure.

`Pressure switches`:

- detective asks for immediate collection of a dramatic object
- building staff pushes for quick corridor reopening
- phone video from a bystander begins circulating online

### Technician Variant 2. Scene Graph Honesty in a Quiet Office

`Base scenario`: Exercise 2. Office Quiet Death

`Role exposure setup`:

The technician inherits a still, tidy office where very little looks disturbed. Digital devices, desk materials, entry points, seating position, and environmental controls may matter more than overt physical disruption.

`Training objective`:

Force the learner to build a graph around low-drama structure instead of waiting for dramatic payload.

`Learner tasks`:

- identify nodes and edges that matter even if they look mundane
- distinguish visible order from analytical order
- map possible propagation or access paths without overclaiming sequence
- identify what should be handed toward lab or detectives rather than guessed at scene level

`Required outputs`:

- Form 3 transition map excerpt
- Form 5 scene graph summary
- Form 8 draft discrimination request

`Evaluator focus`:

The strongest answers treat devices, access history, workspace arrangement, and environmental state as structurally live.

Weak answers wait for a visibly violent clue.

`Pressure switches`:

- supervisor asks why the technician is taking so long in an apparently simple office
- manager claims nothing in the room was touched overnight
- coworker offers a detailed speculative story while the technician is mapping

### Technician Variant 3. Ranked Collection in a Warehouse Environment

`Base scenario`: Exercise 3. Warehouse Loading Bay Reconciliation Failure

`Role exposure setup`:

The technician inherits a wide, noisy logistics scene with vehicles, bay surfaces, packaging, digital records, and multiple actors whose movement has already complicated the area.

`Training objective`:

Train the learner to prioritize collection by discriminating power rather than by sheer quantity.

`Learner tasks`:

- rank the first collection actions in order
- justify why each action would split a live hypothesis or preserve a vanishing state
- identify what should not be collected yet
- write the first lab-facing question that would matter if the collected material proves usable

`Required outputs`:

- Form 7 ranked collection entry set
- Form 8 lab request draft
- Form 12 technician section

`Evaluator focus`:

The learner should make the scene smaller analytically, not just bigger administratively.

`Pressure switches`:

- command asks for full collection of everything in the bay
- weather threatens trace loss
- a contractor insists that one pathway is irrelevant because it is “routine”

### Technician progression note

Technician variants should be followed by a blind handoff drill in which detectives or lab personnel read only the technician outputs.

If the outputs do not transfer, the technician has not finished the exercise successfully.

## Part III. Detective Variants

### Role focus

Detective variants should be built to punish witness fusion, motive gravity, and hypothesis laundering.

### Detective Variant 1. Witness Disaggregation in the Hallway Case

`Base scenario`: Exercise 1. Apartment Hallway Collapse

`Role exposure setup`:

The detective inherits patrol notes, early scene impressions, and statements from neighbors that partially overlap but may be socially contaminated. The physical scene appears narrower than the witness narratives.

`Training objective`:

Make the detective separate people, statements, and anchors before allowing a theory to cohere.

`Learner tasks`:

- distinguish witness-supplied sequence from anchored sequence
- list what pieces of each statement remain uncorroborated
- identify where confidence or confidence-matching may be misleading
- propose the next smallest discriminating step

`Required outputs`:

- Form 4 detective anchor hierarchy excerpt
- Form 6 ranked hypotheses table
- Form 9 excuse ledger entries

`Evaluator focus`:

The detective should not flatten multiple witnesses into one composite voice.

`Pressure switches`:

- one witness is highly articulate and socially credible
- supervisor asks for the “most likely” explanation immediately
- media attention makes a simple story desirable

### Detective Variant 2. Ranked-Hypothesis Maintenance in the Quiet Office Case

`Base scenario`: Exercise 2. Office Quiet Death

`Role exposure setup`:

The detective inherits a calm scene with motive-rich context and subtle physical structure. The environment invites natural-cause assumptions, intentional-harm speculation, and workplace-story contamination at the same time.

`Training objective`:

Teach the detective to keep multiple live explanations visible without turning uncertainty into vagueness.

`Learner tasks`:

- rank the live hypotheses
- identify kill conditions for each of the top three hypotheses
- name which anchor conflicts matter most
- write one lab question and one investigative question that would narrow the field fastest

`Required outputs`:

- Form 6 complete top-of-table entry set
- Form 8 detective lab request draft
- Form 10 deferral entries

`Evaluator focus`:

A strong answer makes the field narrower while preserving live alternatives honestly.

A weak answer merely lists possibilities without ordering them.

`Pressure switches`:

- executive pressure to call the case routine
- coworker rumor introduces motive-rich noise
- partial digital timeline tempts premature closure

### Detective Variant 3. Blind Package Transfer in the Warehouse Case

`Base scenario`: Exercise 3. Warehouse Loading Bay Reconciliation Failure

`Role exposure setup`:

The detective must produce a package summary for a reviewer who has not been at the scene. The evidence field includes physical movement, records mismatch, human traffic, and ambiguous operational routine.

`Training objective`:

Train the detective to produce a transferable structural package rather than a charismatic summary.

`Learner tasks`:

- write the strongest presently lawful sequence claim
- identify the most important unresolved contradiction
- state what a reviewer might overclaim if the package is read carelessly
- identify what remains deferred and who owns it

`Required outputs`:

- Form 2 closure-contract excerpt
- Form 9 excuse ledger entries
- Form 12 detective section

`Evaluator focus`:

The package should survive being read by someone who is unimpressed by the detective's confidence.

`Pressure switches`:

- prosecutor requests a same-day review packet
- supervisor demands a clean story for command
- site records are incomplete but politically important

### Detective progression note

Detective variants should often be followed by supervisor review variants using the detective outputs as raw material.

That is where narrative drift becomes teachable.

## Part IV. Laboratory Personnel Variants

### Role focus

Laboratory variants should be built to punish passive intake, generic testing, and overconfident null-result language.

### Laboratory Variant 1. Structured-Question Intake

`Base scenario`: Exercise 3. Warehouse Loading Bay Reconciliation Failure

`Role exposure setup`:

The lab receives a mixed submission with partial narrative framing, incomplete distinction requests, and pressure for fast processing because operations are disrupted.

`Training objective`:

Teach the learner to rewrite vague intake into analyzable structural questions.

`Learner tasks`:

- identify what question the submission appears to ask
- identify what question it actually needs to ask
- reject or rewrite vague testing language
- state what additional context or material would matter before full processing

`Required outputs`:

- rewritten Form 8 request
- Form 9 lab excuse ledger entries
- a short intake clarification note

`Evaluator focus`:

The best answer turns an imprecise service request into a bounded analytical problem.

`Pressure switches`:

- detective confirmation pressure
- queue pressure from unrelated cases
- leadership request for same-day certainty

### Laboratory Variant 2. Null-Result Interpretation

`Base scenario`: Exercise 2. Office Quiet Death

`Role exposure setup`:

The lab receives items from a quiet office case where some expected findings may not appear, and where stakeholders are likely to overread absence as proof.

`Training objective`:

Train the learner to distinguish evidentiary absence, method limit, and regime mismatch.

`Learner tasks`:

- explain three plausible reasons a null result might occur
- state what the null result does and does not narrow
- identify whether reprocessing is justified
- identify what should be returned to scene or detectives for clarification

`Required outputs`:

- Form 9 null-result ledger entry set
- Form 10 deferral entries
- Form 12 lab section

`Evaluator focus`:

The learner should not let the null result become rhetorical fuel for either side.

`Pressure switches`:

- detective wants the null written as support for the preferred hypothesis
- supervisor wants a clean, final-looking report line
- weak substrate conditions tempt overcompression of method limits

### Laboratory Variant 3. Submission Rewrite and Test-Order Redesign

`Base scenario`: Exercise 1. Apartment Hallway Collapse

`Role exposure setup`:

The lab receives a submission that includes a potentially useful item set but a weak request structure. The issue is not lack of material alone. It is poor analytical framing.

`Training objective`:

Make the learner redesign the test order based on discriminating value.

`Learner tasks`:

- identify which testing sequence would maximize narrowing value
- state which tests are tempting but low-yield at the current stage
- rewrite the submission in language a disciplined analyst would want to receive
- name what downstream users must not claim regardless of result

`Required outputs`:

- revised Form 8
- a short sequencing rationale
- Form 12 lab warning section

`Evaluator focus`:

The learner succeeds if they preserve the distinction between technical capability and case value.

`Pressure switches`:

- volume pressure causes a push for routine panel ordering
- public-facing case status increases overclaim temptation
- limited sample quantity makes sequence choice consequential

### Laboratory progression note

Lab variants should end with a cross-role return step in which detectives or prosecutors must read the lab output and identify what they are not permitted to say.

## Part V. Supervisor and Review Board Variants

### Role focus

Supervisory variants should be built to punish approval-by-polish and reward structural challenge.

### Supervisor Variant 1. Closure-Contract Review

`Base scenario`: use outputs generated from Patrol, Technician, and Detective Variant 1 in the hallway case

`Role exposure setup`:

The supervisor is handed a package that looks busy and partially coherent. Several actions have been taken, but it is unclear whether the scene or case phase actually meets a defensible closure condition.

`Training objective`:

Teach the supervisor to challenge closure based on structure rather than volume.

`Learner tasks`:

- decide whether closure is justified
- identify what uncertainty is being carried honestly and what uncertainty is being hidden
- rewrite at least two closure lines to be structurally accurate
- name the next smallest required action before approval

`Required outputs`:

- supervisor-marked Form 2 review notes
- Form 10 escalation entries
- a short disposition note: proceed, hold, or reenter

`Evaluator focus`:

The learner should not confuse motion with progress or packaging with closure.

`Pressure switches`:

- command wants the phase marked complete
- case is drawing community attention
- the assigned detective is strongly attached to the current theory

### Supervisor Variant 2. False-Stability Detection

`Base scenario`: use outputs from Detective Variant 2 and Laboratory Variant 2 in the quiet office case

`Role exposure setup`:

The supervisor receives a package that looks clean because very little is overtly contradictory, but much of its cleanliness comes from under-questioned assumptions and overcompressed null interpretation.

`Training objective`:

Train the learner to spot false stability before it becomes institutional memory.

`Learner tasks`:

- identify where apparent certainty is unsupported
- locate the strongest contradiction that is being visually minimized
- use the excuse ledger to name where the process is quietly drifting
- decide whether redesign review is necessary now or later

`Required outputs`:

- Form 9 supervisory review notes
- Form 11 redesign triggers
- a short risk memo for command or review board use

`Evaluator focus`:

The learner succeeds if they can make hidden instability visible without defaulting to vague distrust.

`Pressure switches`:

- dashboard metrics show good throughput
- executive leadership wants a reassurance briefing
- a vendor tool summary presents a falsely clean picture

### Supervisor Variant 3. After-Action Redesign

`Base scenario`: use mixed-role outputs from the warehouse case

`Role exposure setup`:

The supervisor or board is told that the case suffered transfer fragility and required avoidable reentry. The task is not to relitigate the whole case but to redesign the process honestly.

`Training objective`:

Teach the learner to move from critique to structural redesign.

`Learner tasks`:

- identify the earliest preventable failure point
- name the cross-role handoff that broke
- propose one redesign in training, one in supervision, and one in documentation
- distinguish blame language from redesign language

`Required outputs`:

- Form 11 redesign review
- a short corrective-action sequence
- revised training priority list

`Evaluator focus`:

Good redesign outputs change architecture.

Weak ones merely demand that staff “be more careful.”

`Pressure switches`:

- senior personnel resist critique
- workload concerns make retraining unpopular
- leadership wants to declare the issue isolated

### Supervisor progression note

Supervisors should run these variants before they are trusted with live implementation audit packets.

Otherwise the audit layer will simply become a polished legacy tool.

## Part VI. Prosecutor and Downstream Legal Variants

### Role focus

Legal variants should be built to punish overclaim, rhetorical smoothing, and transfer blindness.

### Prosecutor Variant 1. Integrated-Package Reading

`Base scenario`: Legal Scenario 1. Charging Review in the Apartment Hallway Case

`Role exposure setup`:

The legal learner receives an integrated package and is told to prepare for charging review. The package is not empty, but it contains visible residue and several tempting narrative shortcuts.

`Training objective`:

Teach the learner to read the package as a bounded-claim system rather than as persuasion inventory.

`Learner tasks`:

- identify the strongest presently lawful claim
- identify what remains structurally open
- identify what a charging memo must not say yet
- state whether reentry is required before stronger action

`Required outputs`:

- bounded charging paragraph
- risk note tied to Forms 6, 9, and 12
- one reentry request

`Evaluator focus`:

The learner succeeds if they narrow the legal claim envelope rather than inflate it.

`Pressure switches`:

- command demand for immediate charging decision
- emotional victim framing in briefing language
- witness confidence exceeds anchor quality

### Prosecutor Variant 2. Warrant-Language Discipline

`Base scenario`: Legal Scenario 2. Warrant Review in the Quiet Office Case

`Role exposure setup`:

The legal learner receives a warrant draft opportunity built on a calm scene with subtle structure, partial sequencing, and a risk of overstating what the package actually supports.

`Training objective`:

Train the learner to write sequence and probability language proportionate to the anchor stack.

`Learner tasks`:

- draft a bounded warrant-support paragraph
- identify every sentence that depends on weaker interpretation rather than strong anchors
- rewrite one inflated sentence into lawful language
- identify what clarification would justify stronger language later

`Required outputs`:

- warrant-language rewrite
- short anchor-sufficiency note
- Form 10 legal deferral entry

`Evaluator focus`:

The learner should show comfort with saying less, not just skill in saying more elegantly.

`Pressure switches`:

- deadline-driven warrant review
- executive expectation of certainty
- media-sensitive facts increase rhetorical temptation

### Prosecutor Variant 3. Demonstrative and Transfer Audit

`Base scenario`: Legal Scenario 5. Demonstrative Audit Before Trial

`Role exposure setup`:

The legal learner must review a proposed timeline or demonstrative built from a package that contains anchored and partially anchored sequence elements. The risk is not only factual error. It is overclean representation.

`Training objective`:

Teach the learner to audit legal presentation against structural record.

`Learner tasks`:

- identify where the demonstrative outruns the anchor hierarchy
- identify which sequence elements must be softened, removed, or labeled
- state what residual uncertainty must remain visible at trial-preparation stage
- decide whether further reentry is required before presentation

`Required outputs`:

- demonstrative audit note
- revised bounded-sequence language
- one transfer-risk memo

`Evaluator focus`:

The learner succeeds if they treat demonstration as a claim instrument that must stay subordinate to the record.

`Pressure switches`:

- trial team wants a cleaner story for jury use
- investigator resists softening the presentation
- a strong but weakly anchored narrative theme is emotionally attractive

### Prosecutor progression note

These variants should be run after, not before, the existing downstream legal scenario set.

The legal scenario set teaches the legal grammar.

These variants teach the role to hold that grammar under real pressure.

## Mixed-Role Extension Drills

Once role-specific variants are stable, the following mixed-role drills should be added around them:

- patrol-to-technician blind handoff
- technician-to-detective graph transfer
- detective-to-lab request rewrite challenge
- detective-to-supervisor closure challenge
- supervisor-to-prosecutor package-transfer drill
- prosecutor-to-detective reentry request drill

These are not optional advanced drills.

They are where agencies discover whether the roles are actually sharing one structural language.

---

## Facilitator Rules

Facilitators should enforce the following rules during role-specific drills:

- do not give learners more information than the role would actually have
- do not reward polished summary language over structural honesty
- do not let one role solve the exercise by borrowing another role's authority
- do not collapse pressure-drill failures into generic feedback like “slow down” or “communicate better”
- always ask what the learner made more visible and what remained hidden

The core question is not whether the learner reached the right final impression.

It is whether the learner handled the role's actual responsibilities in a way that makes the next role safer rather than weaker.

## Closing Note

Generic exercises teach the method.

Role-specific exercises teach whether a role can carry the method without slipping back into its old reflexes.

That is the real difference between adoption language and adoption architecture.
