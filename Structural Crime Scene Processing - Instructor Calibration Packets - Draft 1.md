# Structural Crime Scene Processing

## Instructor Calibration Packets

## Draft 1

---

## Purpose

This document provides structured calibration packets for instructors, facilitators, legal trainers, supervisory trainers, and academy leads who are responsible for teaching and scoring the Structural Crime Scene Processing method.

Its purpose is not to teach the doctrine again from the beginning.

Its purpose is to align the people who will teach, score, debrief, certify, and remediate others.

That alignment matters because training systems fail in a very predictable way:

- the documents stay stable
- the instructors drift
- the scoring vocabulary remains the same
- the scoring standards quietly split apart

When that happens, one instructor passes confident overclaim because it sounds professional.

Another instructor passes slow caution because it sounds disciplined.

Another instructor rewards charisma.

Another rewards form completion.

The academy then begins producing multiple unofficial versions of the method while still claiming to teach only one.

These packets are designed to stop that drift before it becomes infrastructure.

---

## What Instructor Calibration Is For

Instructor calibration is different from learner assessment.

Learner assessment asks:

- how did the learner perform
- what score should they receive
- what remediation do they need

Instructor calibration asks:

- did two instructors reading the same packet assign the same meaning to the same evidence of quality or failure
- did they use the same override logic
- did they run the same debrief order
- did they send the learner to the same remediation path

If the answer is no, then the institution does not yet have one training system.

It has one document stack and several local instructor cultures.

This document is for correcting that condition.

---

## When to Use These Packets

Use these packets:

- before a new instructor teaches independently
- before pilot certification begins
- when a new legal trainer or supervisory trainer joins the program
- after major scoring disagreements between instructors
- after appeal or review disputes about pass / remediate decisions
- before scale-up into a new unit or academy wave
- after drift audits show inconsistent grading, debrief style, or remediation routing

Do not wait until the academy has already produced conflicting graduates.

By that point, the disagreement is no longer academic.

It is operational.

---

## Participants

Recommended participants per calibration session:

- two to four instructors
- one lead calibrator or training lead
- one supervisory or certification representative when threshold decisions are central

Optional participants:

- one legal trainer when downstream legal scenarios are included
- one mixed-role facilitator when investigator-prosecutor calibration is included
- one audit or quality representative when institutional adoption is underway

Keep the group small enough that actual disagreement stays visible.

Large instructor meetings often reward consensus language before the real scoring differences are made explicit.

---

## Calibration Rules

### Rule 1: Independent scoring before discussion

Every instructor must score or assess the packet alone first.

If discussion happens too early, seniority and verbal fluency will erase the real fault line.

### Rule 2: Write the reason, not just the score

A score by itself does not calibrate anything.

Each instructor must also record:

- what they saw
- what dimension it affected
- what override logic they used
- what remediation they would assign

### Rule 3: Force disagreement into language

Do not allow early convergence through phrases like:

- close enough
- I see what you mean
- I could live with that score
- we are basically aligned

If the group cannot state the exact disagreement, it has not calibrated.

### Rule 4: Debrief order is part of calibration

Two instructors can assign the same score while still teaching different methods if their debrief order is different.

Calibration must therefore cover:

- score
- override
- remediation
- debrief sequence

### Rule 5: Joint output is required

Every session must end with:

- one agreed score or threshold decision
- one agreed explanation for that decision
- one agreed debrief order
- one agreed remediation route if applicable

If the session ends with only discussion, it failed.

---

## Standard Calibration Workflow

Every instructor calibration packet should be run in eight stages.

### Stage 1: Silent read

Every instructor reads the packet, scenario, or learner output alone.

### Stage 2: Independent score and threshold

Each instructor records:

- raw score
- override flags if any
- final threshold decision
- main weakness
- main strength

### Stage 3: Debrief map

Each instructor writes the order they would use to debrief the learner or group.

This matters because some instructors unknowingly debrief toward insight, while others debrief toward humiliation, justification, or rhetoric.

### Stage 4: Difference ledger

The lead calibrator records all differences:

- scoring differences
- override differences
- debrief-order differences
- remediation differences
- role-pressure differences

### Stage 5: Evidence review

The group must point to the actual packet, scenario, or learner output and explain:

- what supports the score
- what supports the override
- what does not justify a harsher or softer decision

### Stage 6: Joint ruling

The group must produce one converged judgment:

- final score or range
- override status
- threshold decision
- debrief order
- remediation path

### Stage 7: Instructor drift note

The lead calibrator records:

- what disagreement pattern appeared
- which instructor reflex caused it
- whether that reflex is likely to recur in live teaching

### Stage 8: Update rule

If the same disagreement keeps recurring, the lead should decide whether to update:

- teaching script
- scoring guidance
- facilitator notes
- remediation routing language

This is how calibration becomes institutional memory instead of one-off correction.

---

## Standard Calibration Outputs

Every packet should produce:

- independent instructor scoring sheets
- a difference ledger
- one converged score / threshold ruling
- one converged debrief order
- one converged remediation route if needed
- one instructor drift note

If those outputs are missing, the session probably produced polite agreement rather than actual calibration.

## Packet A. General Scene Exercise Score Calibration

### Objective

Align instructors on how to score the main scene-processing exercise set so that narrative polish, vocabulary adoption, and structural honesty are not being weighted differently by different instructors.

### Core disagreement to surface

Instructors commonly diverge on questions like:

- how much residue is acceptable in a strong answer
- whether a polished but overclaimed answer should score above a rough but bounded one
- when a strong narrative should trigger an override rather than a high score

This packet is designed to force those disagreements into the open.

### Recommended source materials

- one learner output from the main case exercises
- the general scoring companion
- the instructor scoring sheets and thresholds
- the exemplar completed forms if relevant

### Packet prompt

All instructors score the same learner output from one main exercise and must decide:

- raw score
- whether any severe override flag applies
- final threshold
- remediation route

### Independent-read questions

Each instructor answers:

1. What score would you assign and why?
2. What is the most dangerous failure in the learner output?
3. Does the output contain a severe override flag?
4. Would you pass, pass with follow-up, remediate, or mark not ready?
5. What debrief order would you use?

### Difference-ledger prompts

The lead calibrator should ask:

- Did one instructor reward fluency more than another?
- Did one instructor punish visible residue more than another?
- Did one instructor underuse override judgment because the total score looked high?

### Required joint output

Produce:

- one converged score
- one converged override decision
- one converged threshold status
- one debrief order
- one remediation route if needed

### Convergence standard

Convergence is achieved only if the group agrees not just on the number, but on why a more polished answer may still deserve a lower ruling if it outruns anchors.

### Facilitator watchpoints

Watch for:

- charisma bias
- jargon bias
- reluctance to use override flags against confident outputs

### Signs of successful calibration

- instructors point to the same structural failure
- override logic is applied consistently
- debrief order converges around method rather than personality

## Packet B. Legal Scenario Score Calibration

### Objective

Align instructors on how to score downstream legal exercises so that courtroom fluency, sharp prose, and professional confidence do not mask overclaim.

### Core disagreement to surface

Legal trainers often split on:

- whether a clean affidavit rewrite is too aggressive or acceptably clear
- how much residue should remain in a useful legal product
- when rhetorical closure requires override even if the memo sounds strong

This packet brings those splits into the open.

### Recommended source materials

- one learner output from a downstream legal scenario
- the legal scoring companion
- the legal instructor scoring sheets and thresholds
- the legal exemplar rewrites and sample memos

### Packet prompt

All instructors score the same legal output and must decide:

- raw score
- severe override status
- final threshold
- debrief approach
- whether the learner needs rewrite remediation, residue remediation, or threshold remediation

### Independent-read questions

Each instructor answers:

1. What exact wording in the learner output is strongest?
2. What exact wording is structurally unsafe?
3. Is the output legally useful but too clean, or both useful and bounded?
4. Does a severe override flag apply?
5. What remediation route fits best?

### Difference-ledger prompts

The lead calibrator should ask:

- Is one instructor grading by legal professionalism more than by package truth?
- Is one instructor tolerating overclaim because the product "sounds like real practice"?
- Is one instructor overcorrecting into vague writing and then rewarding that vagueness?

### Required joint output

Produce:

- one converged score
- one converged override decision
- one converged threshold status
- one bounded example sentence the instructors all agree is acceptable

### Convergence standard

Convergence is achieved only if the instructors can jointly say what level of clarity remains lawful without rhetorical inflation.

### Facilitator watchpoints

Watch for:

- professional-style bias
- overuse of "everyone understands what this means" logic
- fear of bounded writing sounding weak

### Signs of successful calibration

- instructors converge on the same unsafe verbs or claims
- the final accepted rewrite remains useful without overclaim
- remediation routes reflect actual failure pattern instead of general dissatisfaction

## Packet C. Debrief Order Calibration

### Objective

Align instructors on the order and purpose of debrief so that the academy does not produce multiple informal teaching methods under one official curriculum.

### Core disagreement to surface

Instructors often agree on scores while diverging on how they teach the lesson afterward.

One starts with what the learner did wrong.

One starts with their own preferred theory.

One starts with the "right answer."

One starts with learner emotion.

Those differences are not cosmetic.

They shape what learners think the method actually is.

### Recommended source materials

- one scene exercise
- one legal exercise
- both answer-key companions

### Packet prompt

Each instructor writes the first six steps they would use to debrief:

- a weak but fluent learner output
- a strong but incomplete learner output

### Independent-read questions

Each instructor answers:

1. What is your first debrief question?
2. When do you surface the learner's first story?
3. When do you surface anchor failure?
4. When do you discuss residue?
5. When do you discuss remediation?

### Difference-ledger prompts

The lead calibrator should ask:

- Does any instructor debrief toward shame rather than method?
- Does any instructor reveal the answer too early?
- Does any instructor skip the step where learners see how narrative gravity overtook structure?

### Required joint output

Produce:

- one agreed debrief order for scene exercises
- one agreed debrief order for legal exercises
- one facilitator script for opening and closing a debrief

### Convergence standard

Convergence is achieved only if all instructors can run a debrief that teaches:

- first story
- anchor overreach
- explanation-density problem
- residue honesty
- next structural improvement

without collapsing into hindsight theater.

### Facilitator watchpoints

Watch for:

- answer-first teaching
- personality-first teaching
- punishment framing
- charisma drift

### Signs of successful calibration

- debrief sequence becomes explicit rather than tacit
- instructors stop using their own personal rhythm as hidden curriculum
- learner improvement is tied to the same order every time

## Packet D. Override Judgment Calibration

### Objective

Align instructors on when severe override flags should trump otherwise respectable scores.

### Core disagreement to surface

Many instructor drifts begin here.

An output scores numerically well.

The instructor knows something is wrong.

But they pass it anyway because:

- the learner sounds competent
- the form is complete
- the total is high
- the output looks professional

This packet exists to harden override discipline.

### Recommended source materials

- one high-scoring but structurally unsafe learner output
- one lower-scoring but honest learner output
- both threshold documents

### Packet prompt

All instructors must decide whether each output:

- passes outright
- passes with follow-up
- must be overridden into remediation

### Independent-read questions

Each instructor answers:

1. What severe flag, if any, appears?
2. Why is this flag dangerous?
3. Why does or does not the total score matter here?
4. Would you sign off on independent use?

### Difference-ledger prompts

The lead calibrator should ask:

- Which instructor is too reluctant to override a strong-looking output?
- Which instructor is using override as a vague discomfort tool rather than a specific structural judgment?
- Which instructor is failing to distinguish between minor polish issues and dangerous false closure?

### Required joint output

Produce:

- one agreed override decision on each sample
- one sentence explaining the override rule in plain language
- one short warning script instructors can use when explaining override to learners

### Convergence standard

Convergence is achieved only if all instructors can point to the same dangerous pattern and agree that structural safety outranks total score.

### Facilitator watchpoints

Watch for:

- total-score absolutism
- prestige deference
- discomfort with telling a fluent learner "not yet"

### Signs of successful calibration

- override is used less emotionally and more precisely
- instructors become consistent in identifying dangerous false closure
- pass decisions become safer without becoming arbitrary

## Packet E. Remediation Routing Calibration

### Objective

Align instructors on how to assign remediation by failure pattern rather than by generic repetition.

### Core disagreement to surface

Two instructors may agree that a learner failed and still send them down completely different paths.

One says:

- run the same exercise again

Another says:

- do anchor drills

Another says:

- rewrite the report

That divergence slows learning and hides which failure pattern the academy actually thinks it is treating.

### Recommended source materials

- one learner output with two to three identifiable weak dimensions
- the routing guides from the general and legal threshold tools

### Packet prompt

Each instructor must decide:

- the primary failure pattern
- the secondary failure pattern
- the correct remediation sequence
- the re-run condition

### Independent-read questions

Each instructor answers:

1. What is the primary weak dimension?
2. What is the secondary weak dimension?
3. What remediation would you assign first?
4. What evidence would show the learner is ready to re-run?

### Difference-ledger prompts

The lead calibrator should ask:

- Is any instructor defaulting to repetition instead of targeted routing?
- Is any instructor confusing symptom with cause?
- Is any instructor assigning remediation that actually reinforces the original failure?

### Required joint output

Produce:

- one primary remediation route
- one secondary follow-up route
- one clear re-run trigger

### Convergence standard

Convergence is achieved only if instructors can name the actual failure pattern and route it consistently.

### Facilitator watchpoints

Watch for:

- generic "try again" remediation
- overcomplicated remediation stacks
- remediation that rewards cleaner storytelling rather than stronger structure

### Signs of successful calibration

- instructors use the same failure vocabulary
- remediation becomes more precise and shorter
- learners are re-run only after the right weakness was addressed

## Packet F. Mixed-Role Facilitation Calibration

### Objective

Align instructors who will run mixed investigator-prosecutor calibration sessions so that they do not accidentally let hierarchy, role prestige, or rhetorical fluency dominate the room.

### Core disagreement to surface

Facilitators often say they will "keep the room balanced."

That is too vague.

The real calibration question is whether the facilitator can:

- force independent first positions
- preserve disagreement long enough to understand it
- stop compromise language from hiding the fault line
- force a joint bounded output at the end

### Recommended source materials

- one mixed investigator-prosecutor calibration packet
- one legal exemplar
- one legal scoring sheet

### Packet prompt

Each instructor must write how they would run:

- the room opening
- the difference-ledger phase
- the forced anchor review
- the convergence ruling

### Independent-read questions

Each instructor answers:

1. What do you say before anyone speaks?
2. How do you stop the most senior person from setting the frame?
3. How do you force disagreement into language?
4. How do you know when the group has actually converged?

### Difference-ledger prompts

The lead calibrator should ask:

- Is any facilitator too passive with prestige dynamics?
- Is any facilitator too eager to smooth disagreement?
- Is any facilitator allowing discussion to replace written output?

### Required joint output

Produce:

- one opening script
- one difference-ledger script
- one convergence rule
- one failure rule for when the session should be marked not calibrated

### Convergence standard

Convergence is achieved only if facilitators can run a mixed-role session without letting investigator experience, prosecutorial confidence, or supervisory impatience silently set the truth standard.

### Facilitator watchpoints

Watch for:

- hierarchy drift
- early summary by the most fluent speaker
- vague middle-ground language masquerading as alignment

### Signs of successful calibration

- facilitators produce similar room structure
- disagreement is made explicit rather than socially managed away
- every session ends with a joint written output

## Lead Calibrator Failure Modes

### Treating agreement as more important than clarity

If the room leaves politely aligned but the real scoring difference was never written down, the session failed.

### Letting senior instructors dominate by reputation

The point is not to discover who is most experienced.

The point is to discover whether the academy has one standard.

### Using calibration as informal policy making without recording changes

If the group adjusts a rule but nobody writes it, the next session will repeat the disagreement.

### Overcorrecting into rigidity

Calibration should create consistency, not dead teaching.

The goal is one standard, not one personality.

### Failing to distinguish scene-training and legal-training drift

Some instructor disagreements are general.

Some are specific to downstream legal work.

If those get blended, the wrong tool will be used to solve the wrong problem.

## Recommended Use Sequence

Run these packets in this order:

1. Packet A. General Scene Exercise Score Calibration
2. Packet D. Override Judgment Calibration
3. Packet E. Remediation Routing Calibration
4. Packet C. Debrief Order Calibration
5. Packet B. Legal Scenario Score Calibration
6. Packet F. Mixed-Role Facilitation Calibration

This order works because it stabilizes:

- basic scoring
- then override safety
- then remediation
- then debrief structure
- then downstream legal scoring
- then mixed-role facilitation

That is the sequence most likely to produce one academy rather than several local teaching cultures.

## Closing Note

An institution can own excellent doctrine and still teach it badly.

That usually happens because instructor drift feels harmless at first.

One teacher is a little tougher.

One is a little more charismatic.

One is a little more practical.

One is a little more legalistic.

Individually, those differences seem manageable.

Across a whole academy, they become alternate operating systems.

These packets exist to stop that fragmentation before it becomes normal.
