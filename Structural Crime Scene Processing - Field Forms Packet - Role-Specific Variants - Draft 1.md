# Structural Crime Scene Processing

## Field Forms Packet

## Role-Specific Variants

## Draft 1

---

## Purpose

The base field-forms packet is intentionally role-agnostic.

That is a strength, not a defect.

The packet is supposed to preserve one structural grammar across patrol, scene processing, laboratory work, supervisory review, and downstream legal handling.

But one packet is used by people standing under very different pressures.

Patrol is exposed to urgency, scene instability, and early contamination risk.

Scene technicians are exposed to visual overload, collection habit, and packaging drift.

Detectives are exposed to narrative gravity, witness fusion, and premature theory ownership.

Laboratory personnel are exposed to service-role distortion, ambiguous requests, and false finality around null results.

Supervisors are exposed to throughput pressure, reward distortion, and false stability dressed up as closure.

Prosecutors and downstream legal teams are exposed to rhetorical inflation, legal threshold compression, and transfer fragility.

This companion exists so each role can use the same forms without quietly mutating them into role-specific narrative devices.

---

## How to Use This Companion

Use this document on top of `Structural Crime Scene Processing - Field Forms Packet - Draft 1.md`.

Do not treat this companion as a replacement packet.

The base packet stays fixed.

This companion tells each role:

- which forms are usually core for that role
- which forms are typically support or review tools
- what each role should emphasize when completing the forms
- what that role should explicitly avoid adding to the forms
- what recurrent failure patterns should be watched for during review

If a local agency wants to produce customized packets later, this document should be treated as the design boundary for that work.

The point is to vary emphasis without deleting the shared method.

---

## Fixed-Core Rules

Before any local or role-specific adaptation is approved, the following rules stay fixed:

1. No role may convert a form into a narrative summary sheet.
2. No role may delete unresolved-state fields simply because the role prefers certainty language.
3. No role may remove ownership fields from deferrals, lab requests, or package transfer documents.
4. No role may collapse anchor hierarchy into undifferentiated "facts."
5. No role may substitute testimony for stronger machine, instrument, or measured anchors without saying so explicitly.
6. No role may use the forms to launder guesswork into administrative completion.
7. Local shorthand is acceptable only if the structural categories remain intact.

If a role-specific variant makes the work feel easier because it asks fewer hard questions, it is probably damaging the method.

---

## Variant Labels

This companion uses three role labels for each form:

- `Core`: this form should usually be actively completed or owned by the role
- `Support`: this form is commonly contributed to by the role even if another role formally owns the document
- `Review`: this form should usually be read, challenged, or used as an intake constraint by the role

These labels do not erase case complexity.

They simply identify the normal center of gravity for each role.

## Patrol and First Response Variants

### The patrol problem

Patrol does not usually destroy diagnosability through bad intent.

Patrol usually destroys it through urgency, convenience, and invisible early decisions that never become part of the record.

Patrol variants should therefore emphasize:

- boundary honesty
- life-safety versus analysis separation
- perishable-state logging
- access control
- explicit handoff of unresolved scene damage

Patrol variants must not become mini-investigative narratives.

### Patrol core forms

- Form 1. Initial Scene Boundary and Stabilization Sheet
- Form 2. Scene Phase Closure Contract
- Form 3. State and Transition Map
- Form 4. Anchor Hierarchy Sheet
- Form 10. Deferral Ownership Table
- Form 12. Integrated Scene Package Cover Sheet

### Patrol variant prompts by form

#### Form 1 variant for patrol

Patrol should emphasize:

- what boundary existed on arrival versus what boundary was created
- who entered before control existed
- what emergency actions changed the scene
- what perishable states were visible but not yet measured
- which access points remain uncertain

Patrol should not use this form to explain why an event happened.

It should document what had to be touched, moved, opened, separated, or left uncontrolled in the first minutes.

#### Form 2 variant for patrol

Patrol closure should mean:

- immediate threat controlled
- medical necessity separated from investigative convenience
- scene authority clearly transferred or explicitly pending
- open risks handed forward by name

Patrol should not mark its phase as "complete" simply because specialized personnel have been requested.

The form should show what patrol has made more diagnosable and what patrol may already have made less diagnosable.

#### Form 3 variant for patrol

Patrol should map only the earliest visible state changes:

- who was where on arrival
- what was open, broken, active, warm, wet, running, lit, or sounding
- which changes happened because responders intervened

Patrol should separate observed arrival-state changes from later investigative reconstruction.

This form is a first-contact transition record, not a theory sheet.

#### Form 4 variant for patrol

Patrol anchors should prioritize:

- body position or location as actually first seen
- access-control state
- visible time displays
- dispatch and body-camera timing
- witness separation timing
- responder actions that altered the environment

If the strongest early anchor is imperfect, patrol should still record it and mark its limitations.

The patrol failure pattern is not weak anchors.

It is unrecorded anchors.

#### Form 10 variant for patrol

Patrol deferrals should be explicit about:

- unresolved witness separation issues
- unverified access history
- unpreserved volatile digital or physical states
- outer-area expansion decisions that specialized personnel must revisit

Every deferral should have an owner, deadline condition, and consequence if ignored.

Patrol should never end its role by saying "detectives will handle it later" without naming what "it" is.

#### Form 12 variant for patrol

The patrol section of the package cover sheet should be short and structural:

- first-contact time
- scene status on arrival
- emergency actions taken
- known contamination or disturbance points
- unresolved boundary risks handed forward

Patrol should not use the cover sheet to summarize the case.

Patrol should use it to keep the first minutes from disappearing.

### Common patrol form errors

- writing conclusions instead of scene-state observations
- leaving responder-caused changes undocumented
- treating witness statements as anchors rather than provisional inputs
- calling the scene stable when only the visible center is controlled
- failing to record who had access before control existed

## Scene Technician Variants

### The technician problem

Technicians are most likely to degrade the method when collection becomes its own reward.

The technician variant layer should therefore emphasize:

- anchor ordering before item recovery
- graph logic before packaging
- discriminating value before collection volume
- laboratory requests tied to actual hypothesis splits
- explicit distinction between inherited contamination and newly introduced disturbance

### Scene technician core forms

- Form 1. Initial Scene Boundary and Stabilization Sheet
- Form 2. Scene Phase Closure Contract
- Form 3. State and Transition Map
- Form 4. Anchor Hierarchy Sheet
- Form 5. Scene Graph Summary Sheet
- Form 7. Collection Priority Log
- Form 8. Laboratory Discrimination Request
- Form 12. Integrated Scene Package Cover Sheet

### Scene technician variant prompts by form

#### Form 1 variant for scene technicians

Technicians should read Form 1 as inherited-scene context.

Their additions should emphasize:

- what the technician inherited intact
- what the technician inherited already disturbed
- what boundary assumptions need correction
- where secondary search zones may exist outside the obvious focal point

The technician version of Form 1 is partly an audit of the incoming scene, not just a fresh boundary statement.

#### Form 2 variant for scene technicians

Technician closure should require the form to answer:

- what anchors are strong enough to preserve sequence logic
- what collection work actually reduced uncertainty
- what uncollected material remains and why
- what scene questions were deferred to lab or investigators

Technicians should avoid equating scene closure with item-count completion.

The scene is not structurally processed because every visible item has a barcode.

#### Form 3 variant for scene technicians

Technicians should use the transition map to separate:

- initiation points
- propagation paths
- later contamination paths
- responder-made disturbances
- environmental or mechanical carry paths

This form should help the technician resist focal-point seduction.

The body, broken object, or disturbed room may be where the event becomes legible, not where the event starts.

#### Form 4 variant for scene technicians

Technician anchors should be explicitly ranked by:

- instrument-measured versus visually inferred
- time-locked versus approximate
- scene-native versus responder-created
- direct condition versus interpretive description

If photo sets, measurements, notes, and collected items tell slightly different stories, the form should surface the conflict rather than smooth it out.

#### Form 5 variant for scene technicians

Technicians should use the scene graph to make structure visible:

- nodes for spaces, bodies, objects, devices, actors, and transfer points
- edges for access, contact, movement, visibility, and temporal relation
- uncertainty marks where relationships are plausible but not yet anchored

This form should not become a drawing exercise.

It exists to keep collection and reconstruction tied to a structural map.

#### Form 7 variant for scene technicians

The collection log should rank items by discriminating power:

- what could split live hypotheses
- what could preserve a fleeting state
- what could invalidate an attractive but weak explanation
- what is dramatic but comparatively low-yield

Technicians should avoid "collect everything visible first" unless the scene genuinely requires that for loss prevention.

The normal rule is to collect by structural value, not by visual salience.

#### Form 8 variant for scene technicians

Technician lab requests should be written in decision language:

- what question needs to be split
- which item or trace can split it
- what result classes matter
- what limitations the lab should know before testing

"Process for prints/DNA" is not a diagnostic request.

It is an outsourced hope statement.

#### Form 12 variant for scene technicians

The technician section of the package cover sheet should identify:

- strongest anchors preserved
- strongest remaining uncertainties
- collection logic used
- known scene losses or inherited damage
- handoff risks for investigators and lab

The cover sheet should tell the next role how the scene was structured, not just what was collected.

### Common scene technician form errors

- collecting before anchor hierarchy is stable
- converting the graph into a cleaned-up story map
- sending generic lab requests instead of discriminating ones
- treating inherited contamination as irrelevant once noted
- burying uncertainty in photo volume and packaging completeness

## Detective Variants

### The detective problem

Detectives most often damage structural processing by converting the packet into a theory container.

Their variant layer should therefore emphasize:

- bounded hypothesis ranking
- witness separation from anchors
- excuse exhaustion
- lawful deferral ownership
- reconstruction language that stays subordinate to the record

### Detective core forms

- Form 2. Scene Phase Closure Contract
- Form 3. State and Transition Map
- Form 4. Anchor Hierarchy Sheet
- Form 6. Ranked Hypotheses Table
- Form 8. Laboratory Discrimination Request
- Form 9. Excuse Ledger
- Form 10. Deferral Ownership Table
- Form 12. Integrated Scene Package Cover Sheet

### Detective variant prompts by form

#### Form 2 variant for detectives

Detective closure should answer:

- what hypotheses are still live
- what sequence elements are anchored versus assumed
- what additional steps are justified by uncertainty rather than by habit
- what cannot yet be lawfully claimed

Detectives should avoid treating the closure contract as a progress memo.

Its purpose is to prevent premature case stabilization around a persuasive explanation.

#### Form 3 variant for detectives

Detectives should use the transition map to test sequence discipline:

- which changes are definitely anchored
- which changes are only witness-reported
- which changes are inferred but not yet split from alternatives
- where transition order remains unknown

This form should keep detectives from stitching witness language directly into the event sequence without friction.

#### Form 4 variant for detectives

Detective anchor hierarchy should explicitly distinguish:

- machine or measured anchors
- scene-condition anchors
- witness anchors
- suspect-supplied anchors
- later interpretive syntheses

The form should make it expensive to say "the evidence shows" when what really exists is a layered mix of stronger and weaker sources.

#### Form 6 variant for detectives

The ranked hypotheses table should include:

- current rank
- support basis
- known conflicts
- kill conditions
- smallest next test that would narrow the field

If a detective cannot say what would demote the favored hypothesis, the table is being used dishonestly.

#### Form 8 variant for detectives

Detectives should write lab requests as deliberate splits between live alternatives.

Good detective requests ask:

- whether a trace, timing pattern, residue, transfer, or biological finding separates one account from another
- whether a null result narrows the field or merely reflects method limits

Bad detective requests ask the lab to make the case "stronger."

#### Form 9 variant for detectives

The excuse ledger is one of the most important detective forms.

It should capture:

- where narrative pressure is being used to explain missing anchors
- where delay is being normalized
- where witness confidence is substituting for structural support
- where earlier scene damage is being retroactively minimized

This form is not punitive.

It is a protection against explanation laundering.

#### Form 10 variant for detectives

Detectives should use deferrals to make unresolved work visible across roles:

- digital pulls
- background timeline reconstruction
- lab clarification requests
- recontact decisions
- alternative-suspect work

The form should show who owns the next narrowing action, not just who is "aware" of it.

#### Form 12 variant for detectives

The detective section of the package cover sheet should state:

- strongest currently lawful sequence claim
- major unresolved contradictions
- live alternative paths not yet killed
- key deferrals still open
- downstream risks for review or charging

It should not read like a closing argument.

It should read like a bounded claim envelope.

### Common detective form errors

- downgrading contradiction to "minor inconsistency"
- using witness confidence as if it upgrades anchor quality
- treating lab requests as confirmation rituals
- leaving excuse-ledger entries vague or ownerless
- writing the package cover sheet as advocacy instead of structural intake

## Laboratory Personnel Variants

### The laboratory problem

Laboratory work often looks rigorous even when it is structurally weak.

The weak version of lab work accepts unclear requests, processes whatever arrives, reports a result class, and lets the rest of the system overinterpret it.

The structural version of lab work emphasizes:

- clear intake constraints
- discriminating analysis logic
- method-limit honesty
- explicit null-result interpretation
- visible return of ambiguity to the requesting system

### Laboratory core forms

- Form 8. Laboratory Discrimination Request
- Form 9. Excuse Ledger
- Form 10. Deferral Ownership Table
- Form 12. Integrated Scene Package Cover Sheet

### Laboratory review forms

- Form 2. Scene Phase Closure Contract
- Form 3. State and Transition Map
- Form 4. Anchor Hierarchy Sheet
- Form 6. Ranked Hypotheses Table

### Laboratory variant prompts by form

#### Form 8 variant for laboratory personnel

Lab personnel should treat the discrimination request as an intake filter, not as a passive routing slip.

The form should let the lab answer:

- what exact distinction is being requested
- whether the submitted material can actually bear that distinction
- what method class is proportionate
- what result classes will or will not change the case state

If the request is vague, the lab should push it back for clarification rather than silently converting it into a generic process order.

#### Form 9 variant for laboratory personnel

The lab excuse ledger should make limits visible:

- insufficient quantity
- mixed substrate
- degraded source
- ambiguous comparison value
- method cannot answer the requested distinction

This ledger should also record institutional excuses:

- backlog delay used as a substitute for prioritization logic
- "standard practice" invoked without case-specific justification
- intake vagueness tolerated for convenience

#### Form 10 variant for laboratory personnel

Lab deferrals should specify:

- what clarification is required from scene or investigative personnel
- what additional material would actually matter
- when re-submission is worthwhile versus performative
- who owns re-contact and by when

The lab should not hide behind "inconclusive" if the next discriminating action can be named.

#### Form 12 variant for laboratory personnel

The lab section of the integrated package cover sheet should identify:

- what question was actually tested
- what the method can support
- what the method cannot support
- how null or partial results should be read
- what downstream overclaim risks are foreseeable

The structural lab contribution is not merely the report.

It is the shaping of what later roles are permitted to say about the report.

### Common laboratory form errors

- accepting ambiguous requests and converting them into routine processing
- reporting null results without interpretive boundaries
- failing to distinguish method limit from evidentiary absence
- leaving the next clarifying step unnamed
- assuming the package cover sheet is someone else's responsibility

## Supervisor and Review Board Variants

### The supervisor problem

Supervisors are where the organization either protects the method or quietly rewards its collapse.

Their forms should therefore emphasize:

- honest closure thresholds
- visible unresolved state
- ownership of deferrals and excuses
- redesign after failure
- package review based on structure rather than polish

### Supervisor core forms

- Form 2. Scene Phase Closure Contract
- Form 3. State and Transition Map
- Form 4. Anchor Hierarchy Sheet
- Form 6. Ranked Hypotheses Table
- Form 9. Excuse Ledger
- Form 10. Deferral Ownership Table
- Form 11. Post-Scene Redesign Review
- Form 12. Integrated Scene Package Cover Sheet

### Supervisor variant prompts by form

#### Form 2 variant for supervisors

Supervisors should use the closure contract to test honesty:

- is the phase truly ready to close
- what uncertainty is being carried forward
- what open work has simply been renamed
- what pressure is driving closure right now

The supervisor should resist approving closure because the team is tired, vocal, or administratively due.

#### Form 3 variant for supervisors

On the transition map, supervisors should ask:

- which transitions remain weakly anchored
- where the map is suspiciously smooth
- whether responder changes and contamination paths are explicitly shown
- whether propagation logic has been mistaken for initiation logic

This form is often where overconfidence first becomes visible.

#### Form 4 variant for supervisors

The supervisor's anchor review should look for:

- weak anchors ranked above stronger ones
- witness-derived claims disguised as physical anchors
- absence of timing hierarchy
- summary language that hides confidence differences

The review question is not "do we have anchors."

It is "are the anchors ordered honestly enough to discipline the rest of the case."

#### Form 6 variant for supervisors

Supervisors should use the ranked hypotheses table to ask:

- what would kill the favored hypothesis
- what the team most does not want to test
- whether lower-ranked explanations are truly weak or merely unpopular
- whether the next discriminating action has actually been assigned

This form is where narrative drift often becomes auditable.

#### Form 9 variant for supervisors

The excuse ledger should be read aggressively.

Supervisors should look for:

- unowned delay
- generic references to complexity
- hidden expert rescue
- vague claims that "the lab will tell us"
- places where packaging completeness is standing in for analytical progress

The ledger is one of the clearest drift indicators in the system.

#### Form 10 variant for supervisors

The supervisor version of the deferral table should enforce:

- named owners
- explicit due conditions
- escalation path when deadlines slip
- visibility across scene, investigation, lab, and legal review

If a deferral cannot be reviewed quickly by a supervisor, it is too vague.

#### Form 11 variant for supervisors

Supervisors should treat the redesign review as mandatory after:

- false closure
- major reentry
- trial-transfer fragility
- obvious cross-role misunderstanding
- preventable scene damage

The redesign form should answer what process must change, not who should be embarrassed.

#### Form 12 variant for supervisors

The package cover sheet should be reviewed for:

- lawful claim boundaries
- unresolved contradictions
- live deferrals
- hidden fragility in transfer or testimony
- whether the package can survive personnel turnover

Supervisors should not reward eloquence.

They should reward structural honesty.

### Common supervisor form errors

- treating unresolved state as a morale problem rather than a process fact
- signing off on vague deferrals
- reading polished packages as stronger than rough but honest ones
- using redesign review to assign blame instead of changing structure
- failing to inspect excuse patterns across multiple cases

## Prosecutor and Downstream Legal Variants

### The prosecutor problem

Downstream legal teams most often deform the packet when they read it as persuasion support instead of as a bounded-claim system.

Their variant layer should therefore emphasize:

- lawful claim ceilings
- charging and warrant sufficiency without narrative inflation
- transfer fragility
- explicit reentry requests
- demonstrative discipline tied to anchor hierarchy

### Prosecutor core forms

- Form 2. Scene Phase Closure Contract
- Form 3. State and Transition Map
- Form 4. Anchor Hierarchy Sheet
- Form 6. Ranked Hypotheses Table
- Form 8. Laboratory Discrimination Request
- Form 9. Excuse Ledger
- Form 10. Deferral Ownership Table
- Form 12. Integrated Scene Package Cover Sheet

### Prosecutor variant prompts by form

#### Form 2 variant for prosecutors

Legal teams should use the closure contract to determine:

- whether the investigative phase actually supports the requested legal move
- what uncertainty must be preserved in affidavits, charging memos, and courtroom language
- where additional reentry is necessary before a stronger claim is made

This form should slow legal overclaim before it becomes drafting habit.

#### Form 3 variant for prosecutors

On the transition map, prosecutors should ask:

- what order is truly anchored
- what sequence claims remain interpretive
- where timing ambiguity constrains what can be alleged
- whether demonstrative timelines would overstate certainty

The transition map protects against elegant but unlawful chronology.

#### Form 4 variant for prosecutors

The anchor hierarchy should tell legal teams:

- what foundation is machine-anchored
- what foundation is scene-anchored but less precise
- what depends on testimony
- what depends on suspect or witness admissions
- where the case is structurally vulnerable under adversarial attack

The legal question is not whether the package is convincing.

It is whether the proposed legal language is proportionate to the anchor stack.

#### Form 6 variant for prosecutors

Legal teams should use the ranked hypotheses table to check:

- whether alternative explanations are actually narrowed
- whether omitted alternatives would matter legally
- whether the charging theory outruns the table
- whether the table still contains live branches requiring disclosure or further work

This form should prevent advocacy from pretending the hypothesis field is already closed.

#### Form 8 variant for prosecutors

The lab request form helps prosecutors understand:

- what the lab was asked to distinguish
- what the lab was never asked to distinguish
- whether the reported result can carry the courtroom claim being proposed
- where additional testing or clarification is needed

A strong legal reader uses this form to reduce rhetorical overreach around forensic findings.

#### Form 9 variant for prosecutors

The excuse ledger should be read as a risk document.

It often reveals:

- patched-over case gaps
- reasons certain inquiries were never run
- dependence on confidence language over structure
- institutional habits likely to be exploited in cross-examination

Prosecutors should not punish the existence of ledger entries.

They should punish their concealment.

#### Form 10 variant for prosecutors

Legal deferrals should specify:

- what must be clarified before charge, warrant, plea posture, or trial use
- who owns that clarification
- whether the requested work is truly narrowing or merely cosmetic
- what happens if the work cannot be completed

This form is how prosecutors request reentry without pretending the case is already cleaner than it is.

#### Form 12 variant for prosecutors

The downstream legal reading of the package cover sheet should focus on:

- strongest presently lawful claim
- most important unresolved contradiction
- biggest transfer fragility
- biggest overclaim risk
- immediate reentry needs

The package cover sheet should help prosecutors say less, more accurately, and at the right procedural moment.

### Common prosecutor form errors

- reading structural incompleteness as a drafting inconvenience
- using the hypothesis table only after a theory is already selected
- overlooking the gap between lab result and legal inference
- treating unresolved contradiction as merely evidentiary weight rather than claim-boundary control
- converting the package cover sheet into advocacy notes

## Local Variant Design Rules

If an agency later creates role-specific printable versions of the forms, the following design rules should govern that work:

- keep form numbers stable across all role variants
- keep field names stable unless there is a compelling structural reason to add clarifying labels
- allow role-specific prompt text, but not role-specific deletion of hard categories
- use highlighting, ordering, or callout boxes instead of redesigning the underlying grammar
- mark review-only sections clearly so roles do not assume they are expected to author everything
- preserve a visible handoff path from patrol to scene processing to investigation to lab to legal review

The goal is to make the same method easier to use, not easier to evade.

---

## Training Sequence Recommendation

The role-specific variant layer should be taught in this order:

1. base packet for everyone
2. role-core forms for each functional group
3. cross-role review literacy for adjacent groups
4. mixed-role exercises where one role reads another role's form output
5. local printable variants only after the shared grammar is stable

If local variants are introduced before the shared packet is understood, the system will fragment into dialects.

That would recreate the original problem under cleaner formatting.

---

## Closing Note

The shared packet matters because all roles are handling one disturbed system.

The role-specific variant layer matters because each role experiences a different failure pressure while touching that same system.

The task is not to give every role its own truth standard.

The task is to let every role use one truth standard without pretending the pressures are identical.
