# Structural Crime Scene Processing

## Case Exercises

## Draft 1

---

## Purpose

This document is the beginning of the exercise companion for the Structural Crime Scene Processing project.

Its job is not to entertain.

Its job is to train perception, method, and discipline.

The scenarios are built to expose specific legacy errors:

- anchoring on the dramatic center
- collapsing early into familiar categories
- confusing collection volume with progress
- treating silence as resolution
- treating the most visible weak point as the causal center
- relying on oral explanation instead of explicit structure

Each exercise is designed to be worked with the book, the field manual, and the instructor guide.

---

## How to Use These Exercises

Each exercise should be run in five passes.

### Pass 1: Surface read

Read the scenario once without analysis.

Record the first narrative your mind wants to form.

Do not defend it.

Just record it.

### Pass 2: Legacy diagnosis

Identify which parts of the scenario would most likely dominate a legacy scene response.

Examples:

- visible damage
- emotional witness framing
- familiar case labels
- high-status evidence types
- apparent points of entry

### Pass 3: Structural reset

Force the exercise back into the new model:

- state the closure criterion
- state the current scene state
- name the next expected transition
- list probable anchors
- name likely carriers of explanation density
- identify probable propagation paths

### Pass 4: Ranked action set

Produce:

- top live hypotheses
- highest-value collection moves
- likely inhibitors
- minimal discriminating tests
- explicit deferrals

### Pass 5: Review

Compare the learner's first narrative to the later structural package.

The point of the exercise is not to eliminate instinct.

It is to show what instinct tries to do before method constrains it.

---

## Output Template for All Exercises

Every exercise should produce the following:

- closure criterion
- state and transition map
- anchor hierarchy
- top three live hypotheses
- highest explanation-density carrier
- highest-risk propagation path
- ranked collection priorities
- minimum lab test set
- unresolved residue
- deferral ownership table

## Exercise 1. Apartment Hallway Collapse

### Training objective

Teach learners to separate the visible focal event from the real propagation path and to resist early classification driven by witness drama.

### Scenario

At 6:42 p.m., a tenant in a five-story apartment building calls emergency services and reports hearing "a fight, then a crash" in the fourth-floor hallway. Responding units arrive at 6:49 p.m. They find an adult male lying partially inside the doorway of apartment 4C and partially in the hallway. He has visible head trauma and is unresponsive. A toppled side table lies just inside the doorway. A broken ceramic planter is in the hallway near the stairwell. Several neighbors are in the hall, talking over one another. One says she heard shouting from inside 4C. Another says she saw someone "run down the stairs." A third insists the victim had been arguing earlier in the day with someone from 4A.

Apartment 4C is sparsely furnished. There are signs of recent cleanup in the kitchen sink. A dish towel is damp. A phone lies on the living room floor with a cracked screen. The building has cameras covering the lobby and the first-floor mail area but not the residential floors. The front entrance uses key fob access. The stairwell doors do not. The elevator requires no credential to move between floors once inside the building.

Patrol officers have already separated the loudest neighbors and moved one person from 4A back into their apartment after a brief conversation. One officer says the scene "looks like a domestic gone bad." Another says it might be a robbery because the victim's wallet is not visible. EMS has stepped through the doorway to assess the victim. Rain is beginning outside, and several people entered through the lobby in wet shoes while units were arriving.

### What the exercise is designed to trigger

This scenario is designed to tempt the learner toward:

- domestic-dispute classification
- doorway-as-causal-center reasoning
- overreliance on neighbor narration
- immediate focus on the apartment interior at the expense of building interfaces
- confusion between the apparent collapse point and the event's propagation route

### Hidden structural features in the scenario

The scenario contains several features meant to reward structural reading:

- the strongest visible event sits at a threshold
- access control differs by building interface
- the hallway, stairwell, apartment, and lobby do not share the same resistance profile
- early patrol interpretation is already contaminating the scene
- the damp towel and sink may function as either payload noise or posture signal depending on later anchors
- camera coverage is asymmetrical

### Learner tasks

1. State the closure criterion for the initial scene phase.
2. Define the present state and next expected transition.
3. Build a first anchor list from the scenario alone.
4. Identify the likely initiation site and at least two plausible propagation paths.
5. Name the highest explanation-density carrier currently active in the scene.
6. Rank the first five collection or capture moves.
7. Identify at least three legacy-model errors that should be actively suppressed.

### Instructor note

If learners become certain too early about domestic violence, robbery, or hallway assault, stop them and ask which anchors have actually earned the category. The exercise works only if the early confidence spike becomes visible to the room.

### Debrief prompts

- Which details felt loudest on first read?
- Which details became more important after boundary analysis?
- Did the learners privilege the body location over the threshold geometry?
- Did anyone treat neighbor certainty as stronger than building-interface facts?
- What did learners do with the difference between the key-fob entrance and the uncredentialed stairwell?

## Exercise 2. Office Quiet Death

### Training objective

Teach learners to resist the equation of quiet with resolution and to use anchors and posture to separate clean appearance from analytical closure.

### Scenario

At 7:15 a.m., employees arriving at a small accounting office find the firm's managing partner dead in a locked conference room. The room is neat. A coffee mug sits near an open laptop. Papers are stacked squarely. There is no obvious sign of struggle. The body is seated on the floor against the wall beneath a whiteboard. The office alarm was disarmed at 6:54 a.m. by the victim's credential. Internal cameras show the victim entering the suite alone at 6:56 a.m. No one else is seen entering the front door before employees arrive. There are no cameras in the hallway outside the suite and no camera in the conference room itself.

The victim's phone is missing. The laptop is awake but locked. An HVAC maintenance report shows a brief climate-control interruption affecting the floor from 5:48 to 5:56 a.m. A cleaning contractor serviced the suite the previous evening. One employee reports that the victim had been anxious about a client audit. Another says the victim routinely arrived early and often took confidential calls in the conference room. A third says the victim never used the conference room in the morning because the sunlight made the screen hard to read.

The rear stairwell can be accessed from the tenant floor if the person is already inside the building. Overnight badge data shows no entry on the victim's credential between 9:12 p.m. the night before and the 6:54 a.m. alarm disarm. The building's garage camera captured a vehicle resembling the victim's arriving at 6:41 a.m., but the plate is unreadable. The elevator log shows an elevator stop on the floor at 5:53 a.m., though no floor-hall camera covers that landing.

### What the exercise is designed to trigger

This scenario is designed to tempt the learner toward:

- treating a clean room as a self-resolving scene
- treating credential use as direct proof of sole presence
- treating locked-room appearance as stronger than discontinuity anchors
- treating emotional witness context as motive evidence

### Hidden structural features in the scenario

- the visual quiet of the room can generate false closure
- access, HVAC, and elevator records create a competing anchor field
- the victim's habits are described in mutually inconsistent ways
- the missing phone may be louder than the more important timing discontinuities
- the alarm, garage, and elevator records do not form an automatically coherent sequence

### Learner tasks

1. Write the first narrative a legacy team would likely produce.
2. Explain why that narrative is structurally premature.
3. Rank the anchors by authority.
4. Identify at least two points where silence could be misread.
5. Produce a minimal discriminating test set for the first eight hours of processing.
6. Name the likely top inhibitor classes if closure cannot yet be reached.

### Instructor note

This exercise should be used to teach the difference between tidy appearance and true stability. If the room feels "obvious" to learners, the exercise is working.

### Debrief prompts

- Did learners overvalue the locked room?
- Did they notice that credentials and cameras do not fully close the path question?
- Did they treat the victim's missing phone as payload or as posture signal?
- Which quiet signals turned out to be structurally louder than the scene's visible order?

## Exercise 3. Warehouse Loading Bay Reconciliation Failure

### Training objective

Teach learners to detect false stability, human reconciliation as invisible infrastructure, and the difference between throughput and truth.

### Scenario

A regional distribution warehouse reports a fatal forklift incident in a loading bay shortly after a shift change. Initial accounts describe it as a tragic accident caused by low visibility and congestion. The injured worker is found near a pallet lane with broken shrink wrap, scattered inventory labels, and a forklift stopped at an angle that partially blocks a scanner pedestal. Supervisors say the site has had "minor reconciliation issues" for weeks because of a software migration but that shipments have remained on schedule.

Workers describe a routine in which exceptions are handled by a small group of experienced leads who manually reconcile scanner mismatches and location errors before outbound trucks depart. The warehouse management system shows repeated same-day inventory corrections in the relevant zone over the last ten days. Camera coverage is partial: there is good footage of the main aisle, weaker coverage of the bay edge, and no direct view of the scanner pedestal or the blind corner approaching it. A maintenance note from the prior week documents intermittent delays in scanner sync for that zone but marks the issue as low severity because "operations unaffected."

The victim's handheld scanner is found on a nearby shelf rather than on the person. A lead worker says the victim had been sent to "help clear a mismatch" moments before the incident. Another says the forklift operator would never have been in that lane under normal routing. A supervisor insists the death has nothing to do with inventory systems and says the focus should remain on line-of-sight safety. The outbound trailer for that bay left on time forty-two minutes later.

### What the exercise is designed to trigger

This scenario is designed to tempt the learner toward:

- narrow accident framing
- treating steady outbound flow as evidence of operational health
- treating exception-handling personnel as normal support rather than structural carriers
- isolating the incident at the forklift rather than tracing propagation through the warehouse system

### Hidden structural features in the scenario

- throughput has remained stable under persistent error
- invisible human reconciliation is sustaining operations
- the actual path into the fatal location may have been created by upstream queue and routing distortions
- a local safety event may be inseparable from a larger system-level diagnostic failure

### Learner tasks

1. Identify the likely highest explanation-density carriers in the warehouse system.
2. Distinguish initiation site from propagation permissiveness.
3. Name the false stability indicators present.
4. Rank the first five evidence and data captures.
5. State whether "operations unaffected" should be treated as an observation or a hypothesis.
6. Build a short excuse ledger from the supervisor statements.

### Instructor note

This exercise is useful when teaching scene workers that not all scenes are domestic rooms, street locations, or obvious violence environments. The same method applies to industrial events, and false stability is often easier to see there once learners stop equating continuity with correctness.

## Facilitator Guidance

Do not reveal the "right answer" too quickly.

These exercises are not built around twist endings.

They are built around diagnostic discipline.

The most important learning event usually occurs when the room sees how quickly it wanted to settle into a familiar narrative before the anchors, boundaries, and propagation routes had been made explicit.

Ask participants to keep two records during every exercise:

- the first story they believed
- the final structural package they can defend

The distance between those two is the training.
