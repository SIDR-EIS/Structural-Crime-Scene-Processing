# Structural Crime Scene Processing

## Leadership Implementation Playbook

## Draft 1

---

## Purpose

This playbook is for leaders who need to turn the Structural Crime Scene Processing project from a body of writing into a functioning institutional operating system.

It assumes the doctrine already exists.

It assumes training materials already exist.

It assumes forms, exemplars, field cards, and evaluation tools already exist.

What it addresses is the remaining question:

how does an institution stand this up without reducing it to a pilot that never scales, a language overlay on top of legacy habits, or a reform brand that disappears under pressure.

---

## What This Playbook Covers

This document addresses:

- readiness
- sponsorship
- governance
- staffing
- pilot design
- training sequence
- document deployment
- live-scene adoption
- metrics
- procurement and technology governance
- resistance management
- audit and drift prevention
- scale-up

It is not a legal manual.

It is not a budgeting manual.

It is not a substitute for the doctrine itself.

It is the leadership bridge between the doctrine and operational reality.

---

## The Core Leadership Problem

Most reform efforts fail for one of three reasons.

First, leadership treats doctrine as if publication equals adoption.

Second, leadership introduces new materials without changing what the institution rewards.

Third, leadership mistakes local smoothness for evidence that the old model can absorb the new one harmlessly.

Structural Crime Scene Processing cannot survive any of those conditions.

It requires explicit sponsorship, document sequencing, training discipline, supervisory redesign, and recurring audit. Without those, the institution will keep the vocabulary and discard the method.

## Part I. Readiness

### 1. The readiness question

An organization is ready to adopt Structural Crime Scene Processing when it can answer yes to the following:

- Is there a named executive or command sponsor?
- Is there a named operational owner?
- Is there a lab integration lead?
- Is there a training lead?
- Is there a willingness to parallel-run rather than force instant full deployment?
- Is there a willingness to expose false stability rather than defend current throughput?
- Is there permission to let uncertainty remain explicit in the short term?

If the answer to most of these is no, the institution is not ready for full adoption. It may still be ready for a diagnostic pilot.

### 2. Minimum preconditions

Before rollout begins, leadership should establish:

- a steering group
- a pilot unit
- access to current scene packages for audit
- a training calendar
- a review cadence
- a protected implementation owner with authority to cross unit boundaries

Without these preconditions, reform efforts usually disperse into side conversations and goodwill.

### 3. Baseline audit

The first operational act of leadership should be a baseline audit.

Review a meaningful sample of current scene packages and ask:

- which packages require live narration to become intelligible
- where explanation density spikes
- where categories are assigned too early
- where labs are quietly rescuing weak submissions
- where unowned deferrals disappear
- where calm is being mistaken for closure
- where recurring exceptions have become normalized

The baseline audit is not there to embarrass the institution. It is there to show leadership what current stability is resting on.

### 4. Readiness scorecard

Rate each of the following as `ready`, `partial`, or `not ready`:

- executive sponsorship
- operational ownership
- lab alignment
- training capacity
- supervisory support
- access to current case material for audit
- tolerance for parallel run
- tolerance for visible uncertainty during transition
- technology and procurement flexibility
- audit capacity

Any area rated `not ready` should become a leadership workstream before full deployment is attempted.

## Part II. Governance

### 5. Steering group design

The steering group should include:

- executive or command sponsor
- investigative operations lead
- scene processing lead
- lab representative
- training lead
- data / metrics lead
- implementation program manager

This group should not be ceremonial. It must have the authority to resolve cross-unit problems, approve document use, set pilot boundaries, and adjust incentives.

### 6. Role design

At minimum, leadership should assign the following:

**Executive sponsor**  
Protects the reform politically and prevents reversion under pressure.

**Implementation owner**  
Coordinates the stack as one system rather than as separate documents.

**Training lead**  
Owns curriculum, instructor preparation, and certification thresholds.

**Operational pilot lead**  
Owns live-scene use inside the chosen pilot environment.

**Lab integration lead**  
Ensures laboratory work is treated as discrimination, not confirmation.

**Audit lead**  
Owns recurring drift checks and baseline / post-rollout comparison.

### 7. Decision rights

Leadership should explicitly define who can decide:

- which unit pilots first
- which documents are mandatory at which stage
- what counts as pass / remediate
- when parallel run ends
- how metrics are interpreted
- when a technology layer can be added
- when the reform is scaled beyond pilot

When these rights remain informal, implementation gets hijacked by status and habit.

### 8. Governance cadence

Recommended cadence:

- weekly implementation meeting during pilot build
- twice-weekly check-in during first live pilot month
- monthly steering review after stabilization
- quarterly drift audit once system is established

Shorter cadence is needed early because false confidence accumulates quickly when leadership only sees output, not adoption quality.

## Part III. Rollout Architecture

### 9. Why parallel run matters

Do not attempt full replacement on day one.

Parallel run is essential because it lets the institution compare the old and new models against the same real work. Without that comparison, skeptics can dismiss the method as theoretical overhead, and supporters can overclaim success before the system has actually been tested.

Parallel run also protects the institution from one of the most common rollout errors: demanding loyalty to the new model before leadership has created the conditions under which the model can survive.

### 10. Recommended rollout phases

#### Phase 0: Audit and sponsorship

Duration: 2-4 weeks

Outputs:

- baseline audit
- named sponsors and owners
- pilot scope
- initial communication plan

#### Phase 1: Design and calibration

Duration: 2-4 weeks

Outputs:

- selected document set for pilot
- instructor preparation
- supervisor question set
- baseline metrics and review rules

#### Phase 2: Pilot training

Duration: 2-6 weeks

Outputs:

- trained pilot cohort
- instructor scoring in place
- pass / remediate rules live
- exemplar packets in use

#### Phase 3: Live pilot

Duration: 4-8 weeks

Outputs:

- new method used on selected scenes
- after-action reviews completed
- drift points observed
- early metric comparison

#### Phase 4: Stabilization and scale decision

Duration: 2-4 weeks

Outputs:

- pilot findings
- document revisions
- scale-up decision
- next-wave plan

### 11. Pilot selection

Choose a pilot that is:

- complex enough to matter
- bounded enough to manage
- staffed with credible operators
- exposed enough that results will be noticed
- not so politically volatile that the first inevitable friction destroys the effort

Avoid choosing the easiest cases merely to produce early success. That creates a fragile success story rather than a real test.

### 12. Minimum viable pilot package

For first live use, leadership should require at least:

- the book or manual as doctrine reference
- the instructor transition guide for training logic
- the case exercises for rehearsal
- the field forms packet
- the exemplar completed forms
- the quick field card
- the answer keys and scoring rubrics
- the instructor scoring sheets and thresholds

This is the minimum stack that turns ideas into usable practice.

## Part IV. Training and Certification

### 13. Who trains first

Train in this order:

1. instructors and supervisors
2. pilot scene personnel
3. pilot detectives and case reviewers
4. pilot lab points of contact
5. executive and command stakeholders for review literacy

If the institution trains operators before supervisors, the operators will often return from training into a reward structure that invalidates the method.

### 14. Instructor preparation

Before teaching others, instructors should:

- work the case exercises themselves
- complete the scoring sheets
- calibrate against the answer keys
- practice debrief using the prescribed order
- learn how to identify false closure and hidden confidence inflation

Instructors should not be selected only for charisma or seniority. They must actually accept the method's hostility to premature narrative.

### 15. Certification logic

Leadership should define what counts as:

- classroom pass
- practical pass
- pass with supervision
- remediation
- not ready for independent field use

Use the academy thresholds document as the formal gate. Do not let local prestige silently override it.

### 16. Remediation

Remediation should be routed by failure pattern, not by generic repetition.

Examples:

- weak anchor discipline leads to anchor-ranking and anchor-conflict drills
- weak transferability leads to blind handoff packet rewrites
- weak closure discipline leads to closure-contract and deferral exercises
- weak propagation reasoning leads to resistance-path mapping drills

This is more efficient than simply re-running the same exercise and hoping the learner absorbs the lesson by exposure.

## Part V. Document Deployment

### 17. Deployment order

Do not flood the organization with every document at once.

Recommended sequence:

1. doctrine documents for leadership and instructors
2. exercises and answer keys for training
3. scoring sheets and thresholds for certification
4. field forms packet and quick field card for pilot use
5. exemplar completed forms for review calibration

This sequence keeps the institution from treating forms as the whole reform.

### 18. Mandatory versus optional documents

For pilot operation, leadership should mark documents as either mandatory or optional.

**Mandatory for live pilot**

- field forms packet
- quick field card
- integrated scene package cover logic
- scoring sheets for training events

**Mandatory for training**

- case exercises
- answer keys
- scoring thresholds

**Reference**

- book draft
- field manual
- instructor transition guide
- book architecture

This distinction reduces document fatigue.

### 19. Version control by policy

Even in a nontechnical institution, documents need version discipline.

Each form, rubric, and exemplar should have:

- a version number
- an owner
- a last revised date
- a declared status such as draft, pilot, or active

Without this, organizations drift into mixed document use and cannot tell whether failure reflects the method or confusion about which version was supposed to be used.

## Part VI. Live Scene Adoption

### 20. First-wave live use rules

During first-wave live use:

- require closure contracts
- require ranked hypotheses
- require explicit deferral ownership
- require after-action review
- do not punish explicit uncertainty
- do not allow prestige override of form use

The first wave is where the organization teaches itself what is actually non-negotiable.

### 21. Live-scene support model

Pilot teams should have access to:

- an on-call method coach or implementation lead
- fast escalation path for document friction
- lab integration point of contact
- supervisor trained in new review questions

This support should be visible. If the pilot team feels socially alone, it will hide its problems and the institution will learn nothing.

### 22. After-action review requirements

Every pilot scene should receive a short after-action review addressing:

- where explanation density appeared
- whether the state model was used
- whether anchors were properly ranked
- whether collection prioritization remained structural
- where supervisors helped or distorted the method
- whether residue stayed explicit

These reviews are implementation data, not postmortem theater.

## Part VII. Metrics and Dashboards

### 23. What leadership should measure

Useful measures include:

- rate of explicit closure criteria in scene packages
- rate of named deferrals with owners
- time-in-state for major transitions
- frequency of reprocessing caused by premature classification
- number of packages intelligible without live narration
- recurrence of exception classes
- scoring performance of pilot learners

These are closer to the real architecture than simple throughput.

### 24. What leadership should not overvalue

Avoid overvaluing:

- evidence count
- report speed alone
- scene release speed alone
- low escalation count by itself
- polished narrative output
- tool usage frequency as a proxy for adoption

These are exactly the kinds of indicators that allow false stability to flourish.

### 25. Dashboard design rule

Every dashboard should answer:

- Is the method actually being used?
- Is it reducing uncertainty more honestly?
- Is it increasing transferability?
- Is it revealing false stability rather than hiding it?

If the dashboard cannot answer those questions, it is likely measuring comfort instead of transformation.

## Part VIII. Procurement and Technology Governance

### 26. Procurement rule

No technology should be added during rollout unless leadership can answer:

- what structural defect it addresses
- what existing human reconciliation burden it removes
- what new failure class it introduces
- how it will be challenged
- what document or workflow it changes

This prevents the institution from using procurement as a substitute for implementation discipline.

### 27. Technology review board

Leadership should create a small review function to examine:

- scene tools
- documentation tools
- graphing tools
- evidence management tools
- AI tools

The review question is never just whether the tool is impressive. It is whether it strengthens the method or merely adds confidence theater.

### 28. AI-specific governance

Any AI use introduced during adoption should be explicitly bounded.

Allowed uses should be things like:

- contradiction detection
- residue summarization
- scoring support
- prompt generation for review

Disallowed uses should include:

- autonomous accusation
- opaque ranking of suspect theories
- ambient reliability signaling
- unbounded narrative generation presented as analysis

Leadership must lock this down early, or the organization will be tempted to use AI as a shortcut back to persuasive storytelling.

## Part IX. Resistance Management

### 29. Predictable resistance patterns

Leadership should expect:

- "we already do this"
- "this is too theoretical"
- "this will slow us down"
- "good investigators already know this"
- "we cannot measure all of this"

These are not random objections. They are structural defense mechanisms.

### 30. How leadership should respond

Leadership should respond with evidence from:

- the baseline audit
- pilot package comparisons
- scoring data
- hidden-expert exposure
- recurrence reduction or failure visibility

Do not respond with slogans. Skeptical operators do not need inspiration. They need proof that the new method sees something the old one keeps hiding.

### 31. Internal communications

The implementation message should be stable:

- this is not a condemnation of past personnel
- this is not a request for theatrical innovation
- this is a structural redesign
- early friction is expected
- explicit uncertainty is a feature, not a defect

When the message shifts from week to week, resistance accelerates because people sense that leadership itself does not know what it is asking for.

## Part X. Audit and Drift Prevention

### 32. Drift indicators

Leadership should watch for:

- forms present but logic absent
- scores inflated despite weak outputs
- oral explanation returning as the real handoff mode
- early category closure reappearing under pressure
- supervisors rewarding smoothness over structure
- labs resuming confirmation-service behavior

### 33. Quarterly audit structure

Every quarter, review:

- sample scene packages
- sample scoring sheets
- remediation outcomes
- use of closure contracts
- use of deferral ownership
- transferability failures
- false stability indicators

The quarterly audit should result in:

- one training adjustment
- one document adjustment if needed
- one leadership reinforcement action

### 34. Recovery from regression

If drift is detected, leadership should not respond with generalized reprimands.

It should identify whether the regression came from:

- training failure
- supervisory failure
- metric distortion
- document overload
- tool misalignment
- pressure conditions not yet rehearsed

Then it should correct that layer directly.

## Part XI. Scale-Up

### 35. Scale decision criteria

Do not scale because the pilot felt promising.

Scale when:

- pilot teams can produce transfer-ready packages
- supervisors are using the right questions
- lab integration is functioning
- training and remediation loops are stable
- drift is measurable
- leadership can defend explicit uncertainty under pressure

### 36. Wave planning

Scale by waves, not by blanket mandate.

For each new wave, define:

- receiving unit
- local sponsor
- local training support
- required documents
- first review date
- local risk conditions

This keeps scale-up from becoming symbolic.

### 37. The end state

The end state is not a moment where leadership declares victory.

It is an institution in which:

- scene packages survive transfer without hidden rescue
- uncertainty is bounded instead of hidden
- labs discriminate rather than merely confirm
- supervisors reward structural honesty
- documents, exercises, forms, and audits all reinforce one operating logic

That is when the method has ceased to be a project and become infrastructure.
