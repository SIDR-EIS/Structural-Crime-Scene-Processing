# Structural Crime Scene Processing

## Live Implementation Audit Packets

## Draft 1

---

## Purpose

This document converts Structural Crime Scene Processing from an observed rollout into an auditable rollout.

It is written for:

- command sponsors
- implementation owners
- audit leads
- pilot-site leaders
- training leads
- patrol and investigative supervisors
- laboratory supervisors
- prosecutors supervising downstream adoption
- quality and inspection personnel
- steering-group members making launch, hold, recovery, and scale decisions

Its job is to answer a specific question:

how should an institution formally audit whether the method is actually being implemented, whether drift is appearing, and whether a pilot or deployment stage should proceed, proceed with conditions, hold, or reset.

---

## What This Document Does

This document provides:

- formal implementation audit packet structures
- evidence rules for live rollout review
- packet-specific decision logic
- corrective-action routing
- signoff posture for pilot leadership
- a bridge between observation, calibration, and governance

It is not:

- a replacement for live-scene observation
- a substitute for learner scoring
- a substitute for instructor calibration
- a generic compliance checklist
- a public-relations summary of reform activity

The point of an implementation audit is not to prove the rollout is succeeding.

The point is to discover whether the rollout is structurally real.

---

## Why Audit Packets Are Needed

Observation tells leadership what happened in a live scene, a live handoff, or a live review meeting.

Scoring tells leadership how a learner or team performed in an exercise or evaluated event.

Calibration tells leadership whether instructors, investigators, prosecutors, and supervisors are operating with one standard.

Audit asks something larger:

- is the implementation actually functioning as an operating system
- what part of the system is strongest
- what part is weakest
- what is still personality-dependent
- what is being hidden by local fluency
- whether the organization is safe to proceed to the next stage

Without this layer, rollout usually drifts into one of three failures.

First, leadership mistakes document existence for operational adoption.

Second, leadership mistakes local smoothness for structural maturity.

Third, leadership continues a pilot past the point where intervention or redesign was required.

These packets exist to prevent that.

---

## When to Use These Packets

Use these packets:

- before live launch
- during the first wave of pilot scenes
- after the first cluster of live handoffs
- after the first cross-role supervisory and legal reviews
- at regular quarterly drift checks
- before scale-up beyond the pilot unit
- after any obvious regression event
- after any transfer failure that revealed hidden dependency on oral rescue or hidden expertise

Do not wait for a major failure to formalize audit.

If the first real audit happens only after damage is obvious, leadership has already allowed comfort to outrun structure.

---

## Audit Principles

Every implementation audit in this project should follow seven principles.

### Principle 1: Audit the method, not the mood

The audit asks whether structural behaviors are present.

It does not ask whether the team seemed calm, committed, enthusiastic, or persuasive.

### Principle 2: Audit against evidence, not reputation

No packet should be closed by senior confidence alone.

If the evidence source is not named, the claim is weak no matter who says it.

### Principle 3: Distinguish adoption from compliance

A form may be present while the logic is absent.

An audit must therefore test:

- question design
- ranking behavior
- residue handling
- transferability
- corrective-action quality

not document existence alone.

### Principle 4: Distinguish fragility from strength

A behavior is not yet strong simply because it appeared once.

The audit must ask whether the behavior is:

- repeatable
- cross-role
- legible to others
- resilient under pressure

### Principle 5: Preserve visible residue

If a packet reaches a positive disposition only because unresolveds were ignored, the audit failed.

### Principle 6: Route findings to the right layer

Every weakness should be classified as primarily:

- training
- supervision
- document design
- governance
- staffing
- metric distortion
- technology or tool misalignment
- role-interface failure

Without this routing, every fix becomes a vague call for people to "do better."

### Principle 7: Produce a decision, not just a discussion

Every audit packet must end with:

- a disposition
- named findings
- named corrective actions
- owners
- due dates
- the next review point

If the packet does not force a decision, it is not an audit packet.

---

## Audit Evidence Sources

Acceptable evidence sources include:

- live-scene observation records
- live-review observation records
- after-action review notes
- scene packages
- closure contracts
- deferral ownership records
- field forms packet outputs
- scoring sheets
- calibration packet records
- remediation logs
- sample lab submissions and result reports
- sample legal-review materials and decision notes
- version-controlled document changes
- steering-group or review-board decisions

Weak evidence sources include:

- general impressions
- informal hallway reassurance
- unsupported claims that "the pilot is going well"
- isolated heroic examples presented as the norm
- confidence that one respected expert "would have caught anything serious"

The audit lead should insist that every important judgment be tied to at least one named evidence source.

---

## Audit Status Codes

Use the following codes for line-item judgments inside the packets:

- `Secure` = present, repeatable, and not obviously dependent on one person
- `Fragile` = present but inconsistent, incomplete, or heavily person-dependent
- `Missing` = absent where expected, or present only as appearance without real function
- `Forced Deviation` = currently blocked by legitimate external or exceptional conditions
- `Unverified` = not yet evidenced by the material reviewed
- `Not Applicable` = the item does not apply to the packet scope

These codes matter because implementation maturity is not binary.

Many pilots fail because leadership treats `Fragile` as if it were `Secure`.

---

## Packet Dispositions

Every completed packet should end in one of four dispositions:

- `Proceed`
- `Proceed with Conditions`
- `Hold and Remediate`
- `Reset the Stage`

These mean:

### Proceed

The audited stage is functioning strongly enough to continue without extraordinary intervention.

### Proceed with Conditions

The stage may continue, but only with named corrective actions, owners, and a short review interval.

### Hold and Remediate

The stage should not advance until specific structural failures are addressed.

### Reset the Stage

The current design is not holding. Leadership should redesign the stage rather than merely urge better performance inside a weak structure.

This is the most important distinction in the whole document.

Many institutions call for more effort when they actually need a stage reset.

---

## Standard Audit Workflow

Every packet in this document should follow eight stages.

### Stage 1: Define the packet scope

Write:

- packet type
- audit window
- units or roles covered
- sites or meetings sampled
- decision the packet is meant to inform

### Stage 2: Declare evidence sources

List every evidence source reviewed.

If the evidence base is thin, say so before judgment begins.

### Stage 3: Complete line-item review

Mark each packet item as:

- Secure
- Fragile
- Missing
- Forced Deviation
- Unverified
- Not Applicable

### Stage 4: Identify dominant fault lines

Name the top three to five structural problems the packet reveals.

Do not bury them in a long narrative.

### Stage 5: Route each fault line

Assign each major weakness to its main layer:

- training
- supervision
- document design
- governance
- staffing
- tools
- cross-role interface

### Stage 6: Decide disposition

Choose one packet disposition.

If the group resists naming a disposition, the audit is not finished.

### Stage 7: Write corrective actions

Each corrective action must include:

- action
- owner
- due date
- evidence required at follow-up

### Stage 8: Set the next review date

Every packet must name when the next audit or follow-up occurs.

Without this, the packet becomes archival rather than operational.

Every implementation audit packet should produce:

- a cover sheet
- the evidence list

- signoff block

Optional outputs:

- a short executive summary

- a document-change request
- a training-change request
- a governance-change request

## Packet A. Readiness and Launch Audit

### Objective

Determine whether the organization is ready to launch live pilot use without reducing the method to symbolic compliance or avoidable confusion.

### Use this packet when

- choosing the first pilot unit
- confirming preconditions before launch
- rechecking launch status after a delayed start
- reassessing after staffing or sponsorship changes

### Primary evidence sources

- readiness scorecards
- steering-group records
- document deployment plan
- training calendar
- certification rules
- supervisor question set
- lab and legal integration commitments
- baseline audit results

### A1. Sponsorship and ownership

Audit whether:

- a command sponsor is named
- an implementation owner is named
- training ownership is named
- lab integration ownership is named
- audit ownership is named
- decision rights are explicit rather than social

Warning pattern:

- everyone supports the rollout in principle but no single person owns the cross-role system

### A2. Baseline visibility

Audit whether leadership already knows:

- where packages currently require oral rescue
- where category closure happens too early
- where labs are quietly rescuing weak submissions
- where residue disappears
- where supervisors reward fluency instead of structure

Warning pattern:

- leadership wants to launch because the doctrine is compelling, not because the current architecture has been seen clearly

### A3. Document readiness

Audit whether the pilot has:

- a defined minimum document set
- mandatory versus optional document logic
- version control discipline
- live-scene observation support
- forms availability
- exemplar availability

Warning pattern:

- leadership distributes too many documents at once or cannot say which are compulsory during pilot scenes

### A4. Training and certification readiness

Audit whether:

- pilot personnel have a real training sequence
- pass / remediate logic is already defined
- instructor calibration has happened
- supervisors are included, not only operators
- remediation time exists in the calendar

Warning pattern:

- training is treated as a briefing rather than a transition sequence

### A5. Supervisory and governance readiness

Audit whether:

- supervisors know the new question set
- review cadence exists
- after-action review expectations exist
- early uncertainty is politically protected
- leadership knows what not to overvalue

Warning pattern:

- pilot operators are told to change, but supervisors still reward the old way

### A6. Specialist integration readiness

Audit whether:

- lab leadership is engaged
- prosecutors or downstream legal reviewers are informed where relevant
- role-interface expectations are documented
- handoff logic is part of launch design

Warning pattern:

- the pilot is framed as a field-only project even though transfer failure is one of the core risks

### A7. Audit capacity

Audit whether:

- live-scene observation has an owner
- audit packets have an owner
- evidence can actually be collected
- there is time in the calendar for review and corrective action

Warning pattern:

- leadership wants audit in principle but has staffed none of it

### Launch decision rule

Choose the disposition:

- `Proceed` if most line items are Secure and no major governance gap is Missing
- `Proceed with Conditions` if launch is viable but depends on short-term corrective actions already owned
- `Hold and Remediate` if critical readiness areas are Fragile or Missing
- `Reset the Stage` if the launch design itself is structurally weak

### Packet A outputs

Required outputs:

- readiness fault-line list
- launch disposition
- prelaunch corrective-action ledger
- named owners and dates
- launch date or relaunch gate

## Packet B. First-Wave Live-Use Audit

### Objective

Determine whether the method is present in live pilot scenes or whether the organization is only performing visible compliance around old habits.

### Use this packet when

- the first cluster of live pilot scenes has occurred
- the first two to four weeks of pilot work are complete
- leadership needs an early continue / hold judgment

### Primary evidence sources

- live-scene observation records
- live-review observation records
- closure contracts
- scene packages from the audit window
- after-action reviews
- field forms
- supervisor notes

### B1. Closure logic in live scenes

Audit whether scenes show:

- explicit closure criteria
- distinction between release and closure
- visible residue when closure is partial
- reentry conditions where warranted

Warning pattern:

- scenes are being released cleanly but closure logic remains invisible

### B2. Boundary behavior

Audit whether first-wave scenes show:

- meaningful physical boundary control
- informational boundary discipline
- interpretive restraint
- authority clarity

Warning pattern:

- tape is visible but meaning has already escaped into rumor and category labeling

### B3. Anchor and ranking behavior

Audit whether:

- anchors are actually ranked
- quiet artifacts can outrank dramatic payload
- collection is tied to discriminating value
- graph pressure is visible in search decisions

Warning pattern:

- forms show ranking language but actual work still follows ritual evidence habits

### B4. Hypothesis and reconstruction discipline

Audit whether:

- more than one live hypothesis remains when justified
- eliminations are named
- sequence claims stay bounded
- explanation density is controlled rather than rewarded

Warning pattern:

- detectives and supervisors revert to a single smooth story under pressure

### B5. Ownership of unresolveds

Audit whether:

- deferrals are named
- owners are visible
- due points exist
- unresolveds are still legible downstream

Warning pattern:

- unresolved items survive only as oral memory or private awareness

### B6. Supervisory effect during live use

Audit whether supervisors are:

- using the structural question set
- protecting explicit uncertainty
- challenging structure rather than demanding reassurance
- improving honesty under pressure

Warning pattern:

- operators use the new method until a supervisor arrives

### B7. Social climate of the pilot

Audit whether pilot teams believe:

- uncertainty can remain explicit
- escalation is safe
- method friction will be used as learning input
- leadership wants the truth more than an early success story

Warning pattern:

- teams are editing their behavior to make the pilot look successful

### First-wave decision rule

Choose the disposition:

- `Proceed` if the live behaviors are structurally real and cross-role use is emerging
- `Proceed with Conditions` if the pilot is real but still fragile in named areas
- `Hold and Remediate` if compliance is outpacing logic
- `Reset the Stage` if first-wave live use shows that the design itself is not survivable

### Packet B outputs

Required outputs:

- first-wave audit summary
- top five adoption strengths
- top five fault lines
- corrective actions before next pilot block
- named review date

## Packet C. Handoff and Transfer Integrity Audit

### Objective

Determine whether the new method survives transfer between roles without being replaced by oral rescue, prestige interpretation, or hidden expert reconciliation.

### Use this packet when

- several live scenes have crossed role boundaries
- transfer complaints are appearing
- oral rescue or clarification traffic is visibly high
- leadership wants to know whether the package is becoming transferable

### Primary evidence sources

- scene packages
- handoff notes
- live observation records
- lab submissions
- prosecutor handoff materials
- review-board notes
- reentry notes where applicable

### C1. Patrol to specialist transfer

Audit whether patrol hands off:

- disturbance history
- boundary posture
- perishable-state awareness
- witness status
- explicit unknowns

Warning pattern:

- the first role gives a category and a mood instead of a structure

### C2. Technician to detective transfer

Audit whether:

- anchor hierarchy is visible
- graph pressure is visible
- collection rationale is visible
- documentation survives without oral rescue

Warning pattern:

- the detective inherits inventory but not structure

### C3. Detective to laboratory transfer

Audit whether:

- the lab receives discriminating questions rather than generic requests
- sequence sensitivity is stated where it matters
- null risk is understood
- the submission does not pressure the lab toward confirmation service

Warning pattern:

- the lab has to infer the real analytical question because the request never stated it

### C4. Detective to prosecutor transfer

Audit whether:

- the integrated scene package is independently legible
- residue remains visible
- bounded sequence survives transfer
- package strength is not upgraded through grammar during handoff

Warning pattern:

- the case becomes understandable only when one detective explains it live

### C5. Supervisor or board feedback transfer

Audit whether:

- supervisory findings become actionable redesign instructions
- open issues are assigned
- review changes are documented
- later scenes show evidence that the feedback loop exists

Warning pattern:

- review creates discussion but no durable correction

### C6. Transferability stress test

Audit whether a downstream reader can answer:

- current state
- leading anchors
- eliminations
- residue
- ownership
- next likely transition

without needing the original operator present.

Warning pattern:

- transfer success depends on one experienced explainer

### Handoff decision rule

Choose the disposition:

- `Proceed` if transfer integrity is materially improving and oral rescue dependency is low
- `Proceed with Conditions` if transfer is usable but still fragile in named interfaces
- `Hold and Remediate` if one or more interfaces remain structurally weak
- `Reset the Stage` if the documentation and handoff architecture itself is failing

### Packet C outputs

Required outputs:

- interface fault-line map
- ranked list of transfer failures
- corrective actions by role interface
- follow-up sampling plan

## Packet D. Supervisory, Training, and Calibration Audit

### Objective

Determine whether the institution's supervisory and teaching layers are reinforcing the method or quietly teaching people to survive review by performing fluency.

### Use this packet when

- the first training cycle is complete
- live supervision is occurring
- scoring variation is appearing
- instructors or supervisors are drifting apart
- leadership is deciding whether the academy and governance layer are stable enough for scale

### Primary evidence sources

- scoring sheets
- instructor calibration records
- mixed-role calibration records
- supervisor observation notes
- after-action reviews
- remediation logs
- pass / remediate decisions

### D1. Supervisory question discipline

Audit whether supervisors consistently ask:

- current state
- closure criterion
- live unresolveds
- deferral ownership
- what would break the leading hypothesis

Warning pattern:

- supervisors still ask first for theory, motive, certainty, speed, or wrap date

### D2. Reward structure alignment

Audit whether teams are materially rewarded for:

- structural honesty
- visible residue
- transferability
- lawful boundedness

rather than for:

- polished certainty
- low-friction updates
- scene speed alone
- narrative performance

Warning pattern:

- staff say uncertainty is allowed, but their behavior shows they do not believe it

### D3. Scoring integrity

Audit whether:

- scores match output quality
- inflated scoring is not masking weak work
- override use is disciplined
- thresholds are being applied consistently

Warning pattern:

- scores look healthy while packages remain dependent on oral rescue

### D4. Calibration integrity

Audit whether:

- instructor calibration is recurring
- mixed investigator-prosecutor calibration is recurring where needed
- disagreements are being recorded
- clarified rules are being carried forward

Warning pattern:

- every difficult disagreement gets solved locally and then forgotten

### D5. Remediation integrity

Audit whether:

- remediation routes are specific
- remediation is actually completed
- repeated failure patterns trigger redesign rather than repeated exhortation
- certification logic remains credible

Warning pattern:

- the institution avoids remediation because it is socially uncomfortable

### D6. After-action review quality

Audit whether after-action reviews:

- identify explanation density
- identify drift points
- identify supervisory effects
- produce redesign input
- feed back into documents or drills where needed

Warning pattern:

- after-action review exists only as a ritual debrief

### Governance decision rule

Choose the disposition:

- `Proceed` if supervisory and training layers are producing one real standard
- `Proceed with Conditions` if the standard is mostly unified but fragile in named areas
- `Hold and Remediate` if scoring, supervision, or remediation are materially out of alignment
- `Reset the Stage` if the training and governance layer is teaching the wrong operating system

### Packet D outputs

Required outputs:

- supervisory drift map
- instructor drift map
- scoring integrity judgment
- remediation reliability judgment
- governance corrective-action ledger

## Packet E. Laboratory and Downstream Legal Alignment Audit

### Objective

Determine whether the specialist downstream layers are strengthening boundedness and discrimination or pressuring the system back toward confirmation service and rhetorical overclaim.

### Use this packet when

- live scenes are now reaching the laboratory regularly
- prosecutors are reading integrated packages
- charging and warrant reviews are occurring
- transfer problems are appearing downstream
- leadership needs to know whether the method survives beyond the field

### Primary evidence sources

- lab submissions
- result reports
- live legal-review observation records
- charging notes
- warrant drafts or review notes
- transfer-risk memos
- reentry notes
- legal scenario or live case comparisons where appropriate

### E1. Laboratory intake alignment

Audit whether:

- submissions communicate discriminating value
- the lab asks clarifying questions when needed
- vague service framing is resisted
- distinction-preservation needs are visible

Warning pattern:

- the lab accepts generic intake because it has learned to decode weak upstream work informally

### E2. Laboratory result posture

Audit whether:

- nulls are bounded
- regime mismatch is considered
- the lab resists confirmation-service pressure
- reprocessing decisions are reasoned rather than habitual or political

Warning pattern:

- result language quietly does more narrative work than the actual result justifies

### E3. Legal intake posture

Audit whether:

- prosecutors read for anchors, residue, and transferability first
- the package is not treated as a finished story on arrival
- legal review does not punish visible incompleteness dishonestly

Warning pattern:

- downstream legal reviewers ask for a cleaner story before they ask what the package actually supports

### E4. Charging and warrant boundedness

Audit whether:

- charging posture matches actual package strength
- affidavit language stays disciplined
- unresolveds that materially matter remain visible
- legal grammar does not silently overupgrade certainty

Warning pattern:

- clarity is obtained by deleting lawful limitation

### E5. Demonstrative and trial-preparation discipline

Audit whether:

- demonstratives track the package
- witness preparation respects structural limits
- transfer to court does not erase residue that still matters

Warning pattern:

- a tidy presentation is being built on a less tidy file

### E6. Downstream effect on upstream honesty

Audit whether downstream behavior is causing upstream teams to:

- inflate certainty
- hide residue
- smooth sequence
- suppress reentry triggers

or instead causing them to:

- preserve boundedness
- improve transfer quality
- document unresolveds more honestly

Warning pattern:

- field teams openly say they cannot present the truth as written because downstream review will reject it

### Specialist-alignment decision rule

Choose the disposition:

- `Proceed` if downstream layers are strengthening boundedness and discriminating work
- `Proceed with Conditions` if downstream adoption is real but still role-dependent
- `Hold and Remediate` if lab or legal layers are materially reintroducing the old model
- `Reset the Stage` if downstream operating logic is incompatible with the current deployment design

### Packet E outputs

Required outputs:

- lab-alignment judgment
- downstream legal-alignment judgment
- top downstream distortion pressures
- corrective-action plan by specialist layer

## Packet F. Quarterly Drift and Recovery Audit

### Objective

Determine whether the rollout is stabilizing into infrastructure or regressing into a vocabulary overlay on top of legacy habits.

### Use this packet when

- the pilot has survived its early live phase
- quarterly review is due
- leadership senses local smoothness but wants proof
- drift or regression has been reported

### Primary evidence sources

- sample scene packages across the quarter
- sample observation records
- sample scoring sheets
- sample calibration records
- remediation completion records
- transfer failures
- exception classes
- dashboard measures

### F1. Forms versus logic

Audit whether:

- forms remain meaning-bearing
- closure contracts are real
- deferral ownership remains real
- package structure is still stronger than narrative polish

Warning pattern:

- all documents are present but the old logic is clearly back

### F2. Oral rescue dependency

Audit whether:

- packages remain readable without live rescue
- hidden experts are less necessary than before
- transferability is improving

Warning pattern:

- one or two veteran operators are carrying the real logic for everyone else

### F3. Category-closure drift

Audit whether:

- early classification pressure is still being resisted
- explanation density is being caught
- supervisors still protect boundedness under pressure

Warning pattern:

- scenes look smoother because premature closure has silently returned

### F4. Laboratory and legal drift

Audit whether:

- labs are still reading structurally
- null humility remains visible
- downstream legal review still protects boundedness
- demonstrative discipline remains active

Warning pattern:

- specialist layers are drifting back toward service roles

### F5. Score and threshold drift

Audit whether:

- scoring remains credible
- thresholds remain stable
- remediation remains active
- training outputs still match live performance

Warning pattern:

- the academy appears healthy because scores rose while standards quietly dropped

### F6. Leadership and metric drift

Audit whether leadership still measures:

- explicit closure criteria
- named deferrals
- time-in-state
- transferability
- false stability indicators

and still avoids overvaluing:

- evidence count
- speed alone
- polished output
- tool usage frequency

Warning pattern:

- the dashboard now measures comfort rather than transformation

### Drift decision rule

Choose the disposition:

- `Proceed` if the system is holding structurally and drift is low
- `Proceed with Conditions` if drift exists but is named and containable
- `Hold and Remediate` if drift is material and spreading
- `Reset the Stage` if the institution has resumed the old operating system under new vocabulary

### Packet F outputs

Required outputs:

- drift indicator summary
- regression source classification
- one training adjustment
- one document adjustment if needed
- one leadership reinforcement action
- recovery review date

## Packet G. Scale-Up Decision Audit

### Objective

Determine whether the pilot is mature enough to scale without multiplying fragility across the institution.

### Use this packet when

- leadership is considering a second wave
- the first pilot unit is asking for expansion
- command wants to move from project to infrastructure

### Primary evidence sources

- completed readiness, live-use, handoff, governance, specialist, and drift packets
- pilot package samples
- remediation completion rates
- leadership review records
- local sponsor commitments for the next wave

### G1. Package maturity

Audit whether pilot teams can now produce:

- transfer-ready packages
- visible residue
- bounded sequence
- owned deferrals
- workable reentry logic

without unusual rescue.

### G2. Supervisory maturity

Audit whether supervisors:

- use the right questions
- reinforce the method under pressure
- support after-action review honestly
- detect false stability instead of defending it

### G3. Training-system maturity

Audit whether the institution has:

- stable certification logic
- credible remediation
- calibrated instructors
- enough capacity to train the next wave without degrading the first

### G4. Specialist maturity

Audit whether:

- lab integration is stable
- downstream legal alignment is stable where relevant
- cross-role interfaces are teachable rather than personality-based

### G5. Drift visibility

Audit whether:

- drift is being measured
- recovery mechanisms work
- leadership can name where fragility still exists

Warning pattern:

- leadership wants to scale because the pilot feels successful, not because the system has become measurable

### G6. Receiving-wave conditions

Audit whether the next wave has:

- local sponsor
- local supervisory support
- local training capacity
- document deployment plan
- first review date
- local risk conditions identified

Warning pattern:

- scale is imagined as a mandate rather than as a supported second build

### Scale decision rule

Choose the disposition:

- `Proceed` if the pilot is mature enough and the next wave is prepared
- `Proceed with Conditions` if scale is viable but requires explicit support conditions
- `Hold and Remediate` if the pilot has not yet stabilized enough to multiply
- `Reset the Stage` if leadership is trying to scale a fragile success story

### Packet G outputs

Required outputs:

- scale recommendation
- conditions for scale if any
- wave-2 support requirements
- first post-scale audit date

## Dominant Fault-Line Classification

Every packet should classify its top findings into one or more of the following:

- `Training Failure`
- `Supervisory Failure`
- `Document Design Failure`
- `Governance Failure`
- `Role-Interface Failure`
- `Staffing / Capacity Failure`
- `Metric Distortion`
- `Technology or Tool Misalignment`
- `Pressure-Rehearsal Gap`

This classification matters because institutions often try to solve:

- governance failures with more training
- document failures with more pressure
- metric failures with more technology
- interface failures with motivational messaging

Those are category errors.

---

## Corrective-Action Rules

Every corrective action written from these packets should follow five rules.

### Rule 1: One owner

Do not assign a corrective action to "the team."

### Rule 2: Observable completion

The action must have a completion condition that an auditor can later verify.

### Rule 3: Right-layer routing

If a problem is mainly supervisory, do not route it only to operator retraining.

### Rule 4: Short review interval for fragile areas

Fragile items should not wait a whole quarter for reinspection unless the stage genuinely requires that timing.

### Rule 5: Preserve the original finding

Do not rewrite the fault line into softer language when it enters the corrective-action ledger.

If the packet found hidden oral rescue, the action should say hidden oral rescue.

---

## Signoff Posture

Signoff is not ceremonial approval.

Signoff should indicate that the signer accepts:

- the evidence base reviewed
- the disposition chosen
- the fault lines named
- the corrective actions assigned
- the next review date

Recommended signers depending on the packet:

- implementation owner
- command or executive sponsor
- audit lead
- training lead
- pilot operational lead
- lab integration lead
- downstream legal lead where relevant

If a packet affects a role and that role never signs or comments on it, the audit may be politically neat but structurally weak.

## Recommended Packet Sequence During Rollout

Run the packets in this order unless a local event requires a different sequence:

1. Packet A. Readiness and Launch Audit
2. Packet B. First-Wave Live-Use Audit
3. Packet C. Handoff and Transfer Integrity Audit
4. Packet D. Supervisory, Training, and Calibration Audit
5. Packet E. Laboratory and Downstream Legal Alignment Audit
6. Packet F. Quarterly Drift and Recovery Audit
7. Packet G. Scale-Up Decision Audit

This sequence works because it asks:

- are we ready
- is live use real
- is transfer real
- is governance real
- do downstream layers hold
- is drift visible
- are we safe to multiply

That is the real institutional sequence.

## Common Audit Failure Modes

### Treating packet completion as success

A completed audit packet does not mean the rollout is healthy.

It means the rollout has been examined.

### Softening findings for political comfort

If a pilot is being protected symbolically, auditors may replace strong findings with gentle language.

That is an audit failure, not diplomacy.

### Confusing a strong operator with a strong system

One excellent detective, one excellent supervisor, or one excellent prosecutor can make the rollout look healthier than it is.

The packet must ask whether the behavior is systemic.

### Using audit to punish honesty

If teams learn that surfacing residue leads to negative political treatment, audit will make the system more deceptive.

### Auditing too late

If leadership only audits after scale pressure is already high, the audit becomes an argument over sunk cost instead of a tool for design correction.

### Repeating the same finding without structural change

If three packets in a row identify the same failure and leadership still issues no specific corrective action, the problem is no longer local implementation.

It is governance.

## Closing Note

Implementation fails quietly long before it fails publicly.

It fails when:

- leadership mistakes comfort for stability
- scenes look disciplined but are not transferable
- scores look healthy but packages are weak
- downstream review punishes honesty
- forms survive while logic disappears
- one respected expert becomes the real infrastructure

These packets exist so leadership can see those failures while they are still correctable.

The purpose of audit is not to make the reform look rigorous.

The purpose of audit is to make the reform actually survive.
