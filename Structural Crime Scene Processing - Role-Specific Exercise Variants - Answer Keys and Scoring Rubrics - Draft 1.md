# Structural Crime Scene Processing

## Role-Specific Exercise Variants - Answer Keys and Scoring Rubrics

## Draft 1

---

## Purpose

This document adds the evaluation layer for the role-specific exercise-variant set.

It is written to help instructors, role leads, mixed-role facilitators, and pilot-site trainers do four things consistently:

1. distinguish strong role-discipline from generic structural vocabulary
2. score role-specific outputs without rewarding polished legacy reflexes
3. identify where a learner can speak the method but cannot yet carry it inside the actual role
4. make role-specific debriefs concrete rather than impressionistic

These are not perfect answer sheets.

They are bounded instructor keys.

The point is not to train patrol officers, technicians, detectives, analysts, supervisors, or prosecutors to memorize one approved response.

The point is to train each role to preserve structure under the pressures that usually pull that role back into the legacy model.

## Master Scoring Model

Score each learner or team across eight role-specific dimensions.

Use a `0-3` scale for each dimension.

### Score meanings

`0` = absent, off-model, or actively legacy-driven  
`1` = partial recognition, but weak role control  
`2` = solid structural role performance with some gaps  
`3` = strong, disciplined, transferable role-specific output

### The eight dimensions

1. Role-boundary discipline
2. Structural visibility and state control
3. Anchor and uncertainty discipline
4. Action / narrowing-value discipline
5. Residue, deferral, and ownership honesty
6. Transferability and next-role safety
7. Pressure resistance
8. Role-appropriate output grammar

### Global interpretation

`0-7`  
The learner is still operating mostly inside the legacy role reflex.

`8-13`  
The learner recognizes the new model but cannot yet hold it under role pressure.

`14-19`  
The learner is functionally using the role-specific version of the method, though not yet consistently.

`20-24`  
The learner is producing strong role-specific outputs that can survive transfer, review, and pressure.

---

## Role Weighting Note

All eight dimensions are always scored.

But facilitators should pay particular attention to different dimensions by role:

- Patrol: 1, 2, 5, 6, 7
- Scene technicians: 2, 3, 4, 6, 7
- Detectives: 3, 4, 5, 6, 8
- Laboratory personnel: 1, 4, 5, 6, 8
- Supervisors and review boards: 1, 5, 6, 7, 8
- Prosecutors and downstream legal teams: 3, 5, 6, 7, 8

This is not a weighting formula.

It is a warning about where each role most often degrades.

## Dimension-Level Guidance

### 1. Role-boundary discipline

`0`

The learner solves the problem by stepping outside the role or borrowing another role's authority.

`1`

The learner roughly respects the role boundary but still performs work that is too generic, too omniscient, or improperly reassigned.

`2`

The learner mostly stays inside the role and handles the main responsibilities correctly.

`3`

The learner uses the role boundary as a source of discipline, not as an excuse for passivity.

### 2. Structural visibility and state control

`0`

The learner leaves the disturbed system largely undescribed or collapses visible state into summary language.

`1`

The learner notices some meaningful state features, but not enough to control the next move.

`2`

The learner makes the main state and transition risks visible with reasonable clarity.

`3`

The learner produces a role-appropriate output that makes state, disturbance, and open conditions hard to miss.

### 3. Anchor and uncertainty discipline

`0`

The learner relies mostly on intuition, confidence language, or smooth summary.

`1`

The learner notices some anchors but does not rank them cleanly or protect uncertainty honestly.

`2`

The learner uses the main anchors with reasonable discipline and preserves some uncertainty honestly.

`3`

The learner builds the output around anchor hierarchy while keeping conditional and unresolved elements explicit.

### 4. Action / narrowing-value discipline

`0`

The learner chooses actions, tests, or next steps by habit, drama, or convenience.

`1`

The learner names plausible actions, but not by clear narrowing value.

`2`

The learner prioritizes useful actions with some structural logic.

`3`

The learner consistently chooses the smallest lawful next move that most reduces uncertainty for the role.

### 5. Residue, deferral, and ownership honesty

`0`

The learner hides residue, leaves open work vague, or treats incompleteness as an embarrassment to smooth over.

`1`

The learner acknowledges some residue but leaves ownership, consequence, or limits weakly stated.

`2`

The learner records most residue honestly and assigns deferrals with acceptable clarity.

`3`

The learner makes unresolved state visible, bounded, owned, and hard to launder away.

### 6. Transferability and next-role safety

`0`

The learner's output is charisma-dependent or unusable without oral rescue.

`1`

The output transfers partially, but major logic remains hidden or compressed.

`2`

The output is mostly transferable and gives the next role enough structure to work safely.

`3`

The output is strong enough to survive skeptical review, personnel turnover, and downstream use without hidden rescue.

### 7. Pressure resistance

`0`

Under pressure, the learner reverts immediately to the legacy reflex.

`1`

The learner holds structure briefly, then collapses when time, status, noise, or certainty pressure increases.

`2`

The learner remains mostly disciplined under ordinary pressure but still shows drift points.

`3`

The learner preserves role structure under noise, urgency, hierarchy, and overclaim pressure.

### 8. Role-appropriate output grammar

`0`

The learner produces generic prose, advocacy language, or role-inappropriate output.

`1`

The learner uses some structural language but does not write in the grammar the role actually needs.

`2`

The output mostly matches the role's forms, notes, or review grammar.

`3`

The learner writes in a role-appropriate structural grammar that is concise, bounded, and operationally usable.

## Patrol Variants

### Patrol Variant 1. Dispatch-to-Arrival Discipline

#### What a strong answer should recognize

A strong patrol answer immediately separates:

- life-safety action from interpretive action
- initial visible state from neighbor narrative
- responder-caused disturbance from scene-native condition
- known boundary from assumed boundary

The learner should understand that the first patrol mistake in the hallway case is not usually dramatic.

It is usually invisible scene damage that never gets recorded.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- The first boundary set should include both an immediate control zone and a recognition that the hallway is socially porous.
- Likely disturbance points should include body access, bystander proximity, doorways, sight lines, and responder traffic.
- Medical-touch logging should identify exactly what was moved, touched, opened, or displaced.
- Known-versus-said separation should explicitly demote witness interpretation below arrival-state observations.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

High scores require visible restraint.

If the learner starts classifying the collapse before documenting arrival state and disturbance, scores in dimensions 1, 2, and 7 should drop.

If the learner logs disturbance honestly and hands uncertainty forward, dimensions 5 and 6 should rise even if some tactical details are imperfect.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- documents EMS and patrol disturbance explicitly
- treats witness speech as input rather than anchor
- names open access questions

Weak cues:

- calls it likely accidental or likely assault early
- says scene is secure without logging who already moved through it
- writes a polished handoff with no residue

### Patrol Variant 2. Witness Separation and Narrative Containment

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The quiet office is dangerous for patrol because calmness invites category closure.

A strong answer protects ambiguity, controls scene language, and prevents office hierarchy from becoming a substitute for structure.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- Witness separation should include physical, verbal, and managerial separation.
- Arrival-state risk should include office traffic, device interaction, climate or lighting changes, and desk disturbance.
- Stable-versus-unconfirmed categories should be explicit rather than implied.
- Deferrals should push later interpretation forward without pretending patrol has already solved them.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

High scores require resistance to calm-scene hypnosis.

If the learner treats calmness as evidence of routine cause, dimensions 2, 3, and 7 should drop.

If the learner preserves ambiguity and controls narrative contamination, dimensions 1, 5, and 6 should rise.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- separates manager narrative from scene facts
- protects witness channels early
- names what should not yet be classified

Weak cues:

- adopts office explanation language immediately
- allows scene category to be set by status or tone
- writes deferrals that amount to “detectives will sort it out”

### Patrol Variant 3. Blind Handoff Under Mixed Agency Traffic

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The warehouse problem for patrol is inherited disorder.

A strong answer makes that inherited disorder visible instead of pretending patrol arrived first into a clean space.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- Boundary work should distinguish operational space from investigatively relevant space.
- Already-present personnel should be logged as scene-history facts, not as background noise.
- Perishable-state priorities should include environmental, traffic, and record-linked conditions.
- The handoff should tell the next role what patrol cannot validate, not only what patrol observed.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

This variant is heavily transfer-dependent.

If the handoff looks organized but hides access ambiguity and mixed-scene traffic, dimensions 5 and 6 should fall sharply.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- logs inherited movement through the bay
- names the scene as partially disturbed on arrival
- gives the next role unresolved access and traffic risks

Weak cues:

- treats private security and site staff as analytically irrelevant
- compresses large-scene ambiguity into a neat perimeter statement
- hands off conclusions instead of open problems

## Scene Technician Variants

### Technician Variant 1. First-Ten-Minute Anchor Capture

#### What a strong answer should recognize

In the hallway case, the technician's first job is to protect the anchor hierarchy from the aftermath of response activity.

A strong answer privileges sequence-preserving anchors over visually dramatic collection.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- Immediate anchors should include state, position, timing, and responder-change markers.
- Scene-native versus responder-created distinctions should be explicit.
- Pre-collection measures should include photos, measurements, and relation captures that stabilize later interpretation.
- Low-priority items should be identified by low discriminating power, not by low emotional weight.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

If the learner starts collecting before ranking anchors, dimensions 3 and 4 should drop.

If the learner records inherited disturbance honestly and still preserves usable structure, dimensions 2, 5, and 6 should rise.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- states what must be captured before touching items
- separates scene-native from responder-created change
- demotes dramatic but low-value objects

Weak cues:

- defaults to full collection language immediately
- speaks of “processing the scene” without anchor order
- assumes patrol disturbance can be ignored once noted

### Technician Variant 2. Scene Graph Honesty in a Quiet Office

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The quiet office variant is a graph problem, not a spectacle problem.

A strong answer builds structure around devices, access, arrangement, and low-drama environmental relations.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- Nodes and edges should include people, objects, entry points, timing systems, and device relationships.
- Visible order should not be equated with analytical order.
- Propagation or access paths should remain conditional where not anchored.
- Handoff toward lab or detectives should be explicit where the technician cannot narrow further alone.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

Facilitators should penalize learners who wait for dramatic payload before making structure visible.

High scores require seeing ordinary office conditions as structurally significant.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- graphs mundane but live relationships
- marks uncertainty instead of smoothing it away
- uses low-drama anchors productively

Weak cues:

- calls the scene simple because it looks tidy
- overstates sequence from arrangement alone
- ignores environmental and digital structure

### Technician Variant 3. Ranked Collection in a Warehouse Environment

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The warehouse variant tests whether the learner can make a large scene analytically smaller.

A strong answer ranks collection by narrowing value rather than by surface volume.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- First collection actions should be justified against live hypotheses or vanishing states.
- Items not yet worth collecting should be named and defended.
- The first lab-facing question should ask for a distinction, not general reinforcement.
- The technician section of the package should show the logic of the ranking, not just the order.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

Dimension 4 should dominate this variant.

If the learner responds to scene scale by collecting broadly and explaining later, the learner is still in the legacy technician model.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- ranks by discriminating power
- identifies what not to collect yet
- writes a real lab-splitting question

Weak cues:

- “collect everything first”
- no justification for order
- lab request reads like routine processing language

## Detective Variants

### Detective Variant 1. Witness Disaggregation in the Hallway Case

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The hallway case punishes detectives who let witness confidence harden into pseudo-anchor structure.

A strong answer separates speaker, claim, corroboration status, and conflict.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- Witness-supplied sequence must be separated from anchored sequence.
- Uncorroborated statement elements must remain visible.
- Confidence matching and social credibility effects must be named.
- The next step should be small, specific, and discriminating.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

If the learner produces one composite story voice, dimensions 3, 4, and 8 should drop.

If the learner preserves contradictions without becoming vague, dimensions 5 and 6 should rise.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- preserves speaker separation
- identifies where statement overlap may be contamination rather than corroboration
- chooses a narrow next step

Weak cues:

- merges neighbors into one timeline
- treats articulate witnesses as stronger anchors
- writes a theory before listing conflicts

### Detective Variant 2. Ranked-Hypothesis Maintenance in the Quiet Office Case

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The quiet office variant tests whether the detective can narrow without forcing closure.

A strong answer ranks hypotheses, names kill conditions, and resists motive-rich overreading.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- Hypotheses should be ranked, not merely listed.
- Kill conditions should be concrete enough to matter.
- Anchor conflicts should identify the highest-value contradictions.
- The lab and investigative next steps should be chosen for narrowing value, not for appearance of progress.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

Facilitators should score harshly against pseudo-neutral lists of possibilities.

The learner must show control, not only awareness.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- top hypotheses clearly ordered
- each top hypothesis has a real demotion trigger
- next steps are split between analytical and investigative narrowing

Weak cues:

- “could be anything”
- no kill conditions
- motive language outruns anchors

### Detective Variant 3. Blind Package Transfer in the Warehouse Case

#### What a strong answer should recognize

This variant tests whether the detective can produce a package that survives skeptical reading.

A strong answer identifies lawful claim ceilings and transfer fragility directly.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- The strongest lawful claim should be bounded, not theatrical.
- The unresolved contradiction should be named cleanly.
- Overclaim risk should be identified from the reader's perspective.
- Deferrals should be owned and framed as active future work.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

Dimensions 6 and 8 matter most here.

If the package sounds compelling but depends on oral rescue, the learner has not passed the transfer test.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- writes a bounded claim rather than a neat story
- names the most dangerous downstream overclaim
- keeps deferrals visible

Weak cues:

- sounds trial-ready but structurally vague
- hides contradictions in summary prose
- package would fail if read by an unsympathetic reviewer

## Laboratory Personnel Variants

### Laboratory Variant 1. Structured-Question Intake

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The warehouse intake variant is a service-role trap.

A strong answer refuses vague narrative requests and rewrites them into bounded analytical questions.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- The apparent question and the real question should be separated.
- Vague testing language should be rejected or rewritten.
- Additional needed context should be named with discipline.
- The clarification note should improve structure, not simply ask for “more information.”

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

Dimension 1 matters heavily here.

If the learner behaves like a passive technical service, scores in 1, 4, and 8 should drop.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- rewrites request into distinction language
- identifies what the submission can and cannot answer
- asks for context that would change analytical value

Weak cues:

- accepts the request as-is
- substitutes routine processing terms for structure
- asks for “anything else available” without purpose

### Laboratory Variant 2. Null-Result Interpretation

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The quiet office lab variant tests whether the learner can keep nulls bounded.

A strong answer distinguishes absence, method limit, and regime mismatch without rhetorical drift.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- Multiple null-result explanations should be plausible and method-aware.
- What the null does and does not narrow should be explicit.
- Reprocessing should be justified by potential value, not by discomfort.
- Return requests should be specific enough to matter operationally.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

If the learner lets the null do explanatory work it cannot support, dimensions 3, 5, and 8 should drop sharply.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- protects the boundary of the null
- distinguishes technical and evidentiary meanings
- names specific clarification or reprocessing conditions

Weak cues:

- writes the null like proof
- treats reprocessing as reflex rather than justified step
- hides method limit behind clean report language

### Laboratory Variant 3. Submission Rewrite and Test-Order Redesign

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The hallway submission-redesign variant tests whether the learner can sequence work by case value rather than by panel habit.

A strong answer protects finite material and uses sequencing to increase narrowing power.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- Sequencing should maximize discriminating value.
- Low-yield but tempting tests should be named.
- The rewritten submission should sound like something a disciplined analyst would welcome.
- Downstream non-claim boundaries should be visible.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

High scores require the learner to think like an analytical gatekeeper, not an accommodating processor.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- sequences by value, not convention
- defers low-yield testing deliberately
- states what later users must not claim

Weak cues:

- routine order without case logic
- no protection for limited material
- no downstream warning language

## Supervisor and Review Board Variants

### Supervisor Variant 1. Closure-Contract Review

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The hallway closure-review variant tests whether the supervisor can distinguish visible effort from defensible closure.

A strong answer challenges closure based on unresolved structure, not on generalized caution.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- The closure decision should be explicit: proceed, hold, or reenter.
- Hidden uncertainty should be named, not merely implied.
- Closure lines should be rewritten into observable terms.
- The next required action should be small and structurally justified.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

If the learner approves closure because the package looks busy, dimensions 1, 5, and 8 should fall.

If the learner identifies specific open conditions and names the smallest needed next action, those same dimensions should rise.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- closure reviewed against actual state
- distinguishes open work from renamed work
- rewrites closure language structurally

Weak cues:

- treats activity as proof of progress
- gives abstract “need more work” feedback without target
- signs off because the package feels complete

### Supervisor Variant 2. False-Stability Detection

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The quiet office supervisory variant is about clean-looking instability.

A strong answer can identify where the package feels stable only because contradictions and limits were compressed.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- Apparent certainty should be tied back to its true support level.
- The most important minimized contradiction should be surfaced directly.
- Excuse-ledger use should connect case drift to process drift.
- Redesign timing should be justified, not postponed by habit.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

Dimension 7 matters here because the pressure is reputational rather than tactical.

If the learner caves to dashboard cleanliness or leadership reassurance pressure, that should be scored.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- names false stability directly
- uses the ledger as drift evidence
- treats contradiction as structurally important

Weak cues:

- reassures without analysis
- trusts clean reporting tone
- delays redesign because the package is “probably fine”

### Supervisor Variant 3. After-Action Redesign

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The warehouse redesign variant tests whether the supervisor can translate failure into architecture change.

A strong answer moves from error recognition to redesign in training, supervision, and documentation.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- The earliest preventable failure point should be named precisely.
- The broken handoff should be identified across roles.
- Proposed redesigns should be concrete and distributed across system layers.
- Blame language should be replaced by structural language.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

If the learner's redesign boils down to “be more careful,” dimensions 4, 5, and 8 should drop.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- identifies a specific preventable break point
- proposes redesign across multiple system layers
- avoids personality framing

Weak cues:

- vague recommendation language
- no cross-role handoff analysis
- treats redesign as reminder rather than structural change

## Prosecutor and Downstream Legal Variants

### Prosecutor Variant 1. Integrated-Package Reading

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The hallway charging variant tests whether the legal reader can read the package as a bounded claim envelope.

A strong answer narrows what can be said now and identifies what still requires reentry.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- The strongest lawful claim should be explicitly bounded.
- Structurally open areas should remain visible.
- The memo must-not-say line should be concrete.
- Reentry should be requested only where it could change the legal posture.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

Dimensions 5, 6, and 8 matter most.

If the learner treats residue as a drafting inconvenience, the learner is still in the legacy legal posture.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- narrows charging language
- identifies an explicit overclaim boundary
- asks for targeted reentry

Weak cues:

- reads the package as persuasive inventory
- writes as though all major uncertainty is already closed
- treats residue as background noise

### Prosecutor Variant 2. Warrant-Language Discipline

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The quiet office warrant variant tests sentence-level proportionality.

A strong answer can identify where sequence or probability language outruns the anchor stack and can rewrite it downward.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- The paragraph should match the actual anchor structure.
- Weakly supported sentences should be identified specifically.
- The rewrite should reduce overclaim without becoming useless.
- Clarification needs should identify what stronger support would actually require.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

This variant should be graded heavily on dimension 8.

Elegant overclaim is still failure.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- rewrites inflated language downward cleanly
- ties claim strength to anchor strength
- identifies what would justify stronger language later

Weak cues:

- preserves dramatic sequence claims
- only softens style, not substance
- treats warrant posture as self-justifying

### Prosecutor Variant 3. Demonstrative and Transfer Audit

#### What a strong answer should recognize

The demonstrative-audit variant tests whether the learner can police representation itself.

A strong answer understands that visual cleanliness can become structural dishonesty.

#### Key expectations by learner task

- Overrun points should be tied to specific anchor weaknesses.
- Sequence elements should be softened, removed, or labeled for a reason.
- Residual uncertainty should remain visible.
- Reentry should be requested only where additional work could materially change the presentation boundary.

#### Scoring notes specific to this variant

If the learner accepts a polished demonstrative because it “captures the overall picture,” dimensions 3, 6, and 8 should drop sharply.

#### Fast score cues

Strong cues:

- identifies demonstrative overreach exactly
- protects residual uncertainty
- uses transfer-risk language precisely

Weak cues:

- approves clean but overstated visuals
- treats presentation issues as cosmetic
- ignores where the next reader or audience will overinfer

## Common Grading Mistakes in Role-Specific Drills

### Rewarding general intelligence over role discipline

Some learners are smart enough to describe the overall case competently while still mishandling the role.

That is not a pass.

### Overrewarding polished language

The role-specific drills should punish clean legacy prose that hides residue, handoff weakness, or overclaim.

### Penalizing bounded uncertainty

Good role-specific performance often sounds less certain than legacy performance.

That is a feature, not a deficit.

### Ignoring transfer failure

If the next role would still need oral rescue, the output is weaker than it looks.

### Letting pressure runs become sympathy grading

Pressure is part of the task.

It should explain drift, not excuse it.
